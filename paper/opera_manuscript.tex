\begin{abstract}
At the start of treatment for a lymphoid cancer, a haematologist weighs many
risks at once: treatment failure, infection, cytopenia, organ toxicity and
acute care. The tools in use predict one of these at a time, in one disease, and
most complications and rare subtypes have none. We asked whether one model
learning across diseases and outcomes could do better. A sequence model read the
pre-treatment national register histories of 37,531 Danish adults with 24
lymphoid cancer subtypes and predicted 69 outcomes from 30 days to two years. A
matched design separated what it gained from other diseases, from other outcomes
and from nation-scale pretraining. We tested whether the PHAIR encoder, learned
from Danish population records, could be reused for specialised predictions,
directly or after continued pretraining on the haematology records. With every decision fixed
on patients treated until 2022, the models were evaluated once, on 6,446 patients
treated from 2023, against gradient-boosted trees tuned for every prediction and
the prognostic indices recorded in the registers. The one model was more
accurate than per-task XGBoost in \result{T2_better}{X of Y} disease--outcome
predictions (median AUC difference \result{T2_median}{Z}) and covered the tasks of
\result{narrow_fits}{N} separately trained models. In diffuse large B-cell

\section*{Introduction}
For a patient starting treatment for a lymphoid cancer, the question is broader
than whether the cancer will respond. Infection, cytopenia, organ toxicity and
unplanned admission also shape the course of treatment, and anticipating them
lets clinicians plan monitoring and supportive care and lets patients know what
lies ahead. The quantitative tools clinicians have are prognostic scores. The
International Prognostic Index (IPI) and its NCCN refinement combine age,
disease extent, performance status and lactate dehydrogenase for aggressive
lymphoma \citep{IPI1993,zhou2014nccnipi}, and the CLL-IPI and the revised
International Staging System combine disease-specific markers for chronic
lymphocytic leukaemia and myeloma \citep{cllipi2016,palumbo2015riss}. These
scores guide treatment. The Danish guideline for diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
(DLBCL) uses the age-adjusted IPI to set the number of cycles and the intensity
of the regimen, and the CNS-IPI to guide the work-up for central nervous system
involvement \citep{DLG2025DLBCL,Poeschel2019FLYER,Schmitz2016CNSIPI}, and scores
decide who may enter trials of new first-line therapies
\citep{Tilly2022POLARIX,Langerbeins2022CLL12}.

Yet most scores follow a template set three decades ago: a handful of
measurements taken near diagnosis, dichotomised and combined into points,
developed for one disease and one endpoint. Age alone, grouped by decade,
separates survival more widely than the disease-specific indices
\citep{Brieghel2025age}, and keeping the same measurements continuous predicts
better \citep{biccler2018simplicity}. Under the NCCN-IPI, a patient over 60
cannot be placed at low risk whatever their other characteristics. A model
reading the wider record placed 160 such patients with DLBCL at low risk of
treatment failure, against one by the NCCN-IPI, with comparable observed
outcomes \citep{werling2026dlbcl}. Better prediction matters for decisions that
are already being made.

For many patients there is no routinely used predictor at all. Lymphoid
cancers comprise dozens of entities \citep{alaggio2022who}, many uncommon
\citep{sant2010haemacare}. Dedicated scores for rare settings such as
plasma-cell leukaemia and Richter transformation rest on small cohorts
\citep{Jurczyszyn2018PCL,Tsimberidou2006Richter}, and broader scores such as the
MZL-IPI are only emerging \citep{Arcaini2024MZLIPI}. Treatment complications are
served even less. Apart from tools such as CLL-TIM for infection in chronic
lymphocytic leukaemia \citep{agius2020clltim,Agius2024clltim}, routine risk
assessment concerns disease control and survival. These gaps cannot be closed
one problem at a time, because the reliability of a prediction model depends on
the number of events \citep{Riley2020}. In the cohort studied here, the 24
subtypes range from almost 10,000 patients with DLBCL to fewer than 50 with
amyloidosis, and 13 have fewer than 500. A complication with a one-year risk of
5\% in 500 patients provides 25 events, too few to learn how dozens of clinical
variables modify that risk.

Routine records hold far more than any score uses. Diagnoses, prescriptions,
laboratory results and hospital contacts describe a patient's prior illness and
path to treatment, and tabular models learned from summaries of these records
have outperformed the indices for infection in chronic lymphocytic leukaemia and
treatment failure in DLBCL \citep{agius2020clltim,werling2026dlbcl}, now at
national scale \citep{brieghel2025dalycare}. Each such model, however, learns
one prediction problem in one diagnosis. That is the assumption this study
tests. Experience can come from three increasingly broad sources
(\cref{fig:paradigms}). Learning one outcome across diseases adds patients.
Learning several outcomes within a disease adds labelled tasks for the same
patients \citep{caruana1997multitask}. Self-supervised pretraining extends the source of experience beyond labelled
prediction tasks, learning patterns in recorded histories. BEHRT, Med-BERT,
CORE-BEHRT, MOTOR and SurvivEHR demonstrate this approach for structured
records \citep{li2020behrt,rasmy2021med,odgaard2024core,steinberg2023motor,gadd2026survivehr},
and as Delphi-2M and pan-cancer models have done across diseases
\citep{shmatko2025delphi,placido2023pancreatic,Jee2024MSKCHORD,bjerregaard2025pancancer}.
Whether any of this helps a given clinical prediction is not known. Multi-task
studies of health records report uneven benefits and negative transfer
\citep{harutyunyan2019multitask,mcdermott2020mtl}, joint learning helped rare
phenotypes and hurt common ones \citep{ding2019phenotyping}, studies pooling
lymphoid cancers have each examined a single outcome
\citep{parviz2026dacll,werling2026dlbcl}, and models that share experience
across tasks \citep{zhang2019metapred,liu2023taml,chandak2026everyquery} are
rarely evaluated on outcomes that matter to health systems
\citep{wornow2023shaky}, or on how few labels they need
\citep{wornow2023ehrshot}. DALY-CARE was built partly on the expectation that
modelling lymphoid diseases jointly would improve prediction for each of them
\citep{brieghel2025dalycare}. That expectation has not been tested.

Denmark and haematology make the test possible. A personal identifier issued to
every resident links hospital contacts, prescriptions, laboratory results and
causes of death across the whole population
\citep{Pedersen2011,schmidt2014cpr,schmidt2015lpr,schmidt2019danish}, and
national quality registries record staging, treatment and response for lymphoma,
chronic lymphocytic leukaemia and myeloma with near-complete coverage
\citep{Arboe2016lyfo,Arboe2016coverage,daCunhaBang2016,Gimsing2016}. Haematology
offers many varied, clinically defined outcomes, from treatment failure to
graded laboratory toxicity, with competing deaths, and established indices to
compare against.

Haematology makes the question particularly consequential. Splitting care into
many diagnostic entities and complications leaves little labelled experience
for each prediction, even when the combined records are extensive. Yet the
histories may contain recurring patterns of infection, organ dysfunction and
prior treatment across those boundaries. Sharing could make those patterns
learnable for a rare target; it could also obscure clinically important
differences. The question is therefore how far experience can be borrowed
without losing the specificity of the prediction.

Nation-scale pretraining is the broadest test of that argument: knowledge
learned beyond haematological malignancies might become a shared starting point
for specialised prediction, even when much of the source population has quite
different diseases.
The PHAIR encoder was pretrained at national scale, before learning the
haematological outcomes studied here. Reusing such an encoder would allow the
investment in learning from population records to support many specialised
applications. But breadth alone does not establish clinical value: rare
malignancies, treatment histories and laboratory abnormalities may require
further learning on the target population. We therefore ask separately whether
the national encoder transfers directly, whether continued local pretraining
improves it, and whether it reduces the number of labelled patients needed.
These are tests of reusable clinical infrastructure, beyond whether one model
wins an average performance comparison. A benefit would establish useful
transfer from the broader source, rather than identify which source diseases
provided it: common comorbidities, care patterns and repeated clinical
measurements could contribute alongside disease-specific experience.

Here we test how clinical prediction changes when models learn across diseases,
outcomes and increasingly broad patient populations. In a nationwide cohort of 37,531 patients with 24
lymphoid cancer subtypes, \opera{} (\modelname) predicts 69 outcomes at six
horizons from the start of first-line treatment. Specialist, disease-pooled,
outcome-pooled and joint models trained under one protocol separate the two
axes of sharing (\cref{fig:supervision-grid}), a routed model tests whether each
prediction should choose what to borrow, and the same models initialised from a
local and a national encoder test the value of nation-scale pretraining,
local adaptation and reduced training-set size (\cref{fig:encoder-transfer}). The benchmarks connect these questions to current practice: gradient-boosted trees tuned
for every prediction, with and without the quality-registry variables, and
every prognostic index recorded in the registers, each on its own endpoint. All
comparisons are paired on the same patients and were made once, on patients
treated after every modelling decision had been fixed, in a period in which
first-line treatment and the registers themselves changed. Finally, we show
what the model relies on, and how each of its predictions can be explained.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Population, prediction time and outcomes}
We included adults in DALY-CARE who started first-line treatment for a lymphoid
or plasma-cell malignancy, using linked Danish population registers and clinical
quality registries \citep{brieghel2025dalycare}. The models read pre-treatment
diagnoses, procedures, pathology, medication, laboratory results and contact
histories, with age and sex (\cref{fig:model-inputs}). Quality-registry fields
were used for cohort definitions, outcomes and clinical benchmarks, rather than
as sequence-model inputs. The study was approved by the Danish Health Data
Authority (P-2020-561) and National Ethics Committee (1804410).

The start of first-line treatment was the single prediction time. The intended
use is to support prognosis, monitoring and supportive-care planning, rather
than estimate the benefit of choosing a different regimen. Inputs preceded the
index date strictly; index-day records and outcomes were excluded. We predicted
69 outcomes at 30, 60, 90, 180, 365 and 730 days, spanning disease course,
infection, laboratory abnormalities, cardiovascular events and acute care.
Death competed with each non-fatal outcome. Laboratory definitions drew on
CTCAE thresholds and local rules \citep{nci2017ctcae,nci2025ctcae};
register-derived proxies are not adjudicated treatment-attributable adverse
events. Outcome-specific eligibility included baseline and early post-treatment
monitoring for laboratory endpoints, so these risk sets do not represent every
patient starting treatment. Treatment-line estimation, subtype assignment,
code lists and ascertainment rules are detailed in the Supplementary Methods.

\subsection*{Temporal design}
Patients treated through 2021 formed the training set, those treated in 2022
the tuning set, and those treated from 2023 the held-out test set, evaluated
after modelling decisions were fixed. Self-supervised updates used events
before 1 January 2022. Some test patients' earlier histories could enter
pretraining: this is temporal evaluation of later treatment episodes, not a
test restricted to individuals unseen during pretraining. Rare-code merging
used full-period aggregate frequencies, a transductive preprocessing choice.
Some subtype assignments used diagnoses recorded after treatment began;
sensitivity analyses restricted disease evidence to the prediction date.
Preprocessing, temporal safeguards and late-coding rules are specified in the
Supplementary Methods.

\subsection*{Shared learning and nation-scale transfer}
The local sequence models build on CORE-BEHRT \citep{odgaard2024core}, using a
causal transformer pretrained to predict the next event and fine-tuned for
outcome-specific prediction with competing death
\citep{gensheimer2019nnetsurvival,lee2018deephit}. Outcomes can co-occur; they
are not categories of a single mutually exclusive prediction. Under a matched
training protocol, four supervision scopes isolate sharing: a specialist learns
one disease--outcome cell; disease pooling learns one outcome across diseases;
outcome pooling learns multiple outcomes within one disease; and joint learning
uses all cells (\cref{fig:supervision-grid}). A routed model additionally
weights disease- and outcome-owned low-rank adapters
\citep{ma2018mmoe,houlsby2019adapters,hu2022lora}. Disease-identity and own-adapter
controls distinguish borrowing from access to the diagnosis and additional
capacity. Random-initialisation and frozen-encoder controls assess pretraining
and fine-tuning.

To test transfer beyond the specialist cohort, joint and routed models were
initialised from the nation-scale PHAIR encoder, either directly or after
continued pretraining on haematology records. The national encoder used its own
data extract, vocabulary and preprocessing; cohort membership, outcomes and
downstream selection protocol were matched. These comparisons evaluate the
encoders as delivered, rather than isolate population scale from coding or
training budget. Data-efficiency experiments used matched, nested subsets of
10\%, 25\%, 50\% and all training patients, retaining the full tuning and test
sets. Architecture, training exposure, adaptation and provenance are reported
in the Supplementary Methods.

routing choices; controlled sharing and transfer contrasts retain their fixed

\subsection*{Tabular and clinical comparators}
We trained XGBoost \citep{chen2016xgboost} and ridge logistic regression for
each disease, outcome and horizon using history summaries adapted from our
DLBCL pipeline \citep{werling2026dlbcl,bernstorff2023tsflattener}. XGBoost
pooled across diseases tests whether pooling also benefits tabular prediction;
a registry-augmented version assesses the value of curated clinical fields.
The main tabular and sequence pipelines use aligned register sources but
differ in history windows and representation, so this compares pipelines
rather than architecture alone. Features and tuning are specified in the
Supplementary Methods.

Clinical indices were compared with sequence and tabular predictions in the
same score-available patients, on their original endpoints where available;
endpoint proxies were labelled separately. IPI overall-survival prediction in
DLBCL is the main clinical example, with NCCN-IPI and other recorded indices
also evaluated \citep{IPI1993,zhou2014nccnipi}. Model risk-group cutoffs were
fixed on tuning data to match the index's low-risk observed risk and high-risk
specificity. Test performance reports achieved specificity, sensitivity,
group sizes and observed risks. Endpoint mapping, cutoffs and sensitivity
analyses are detailed in the Supplementary Methods.

\subsection*{Evaluation and interpretation}
One-year time-dependent AUC was primary; IPA, Brier score and calibration
assessed probability accuracy \citep{blanche2013competingauc,gerds2006brier,
schoop2011competing,kattan2018ipa}. Censoring-weighted metrics used identical
patients within each contrast. Paired intervals came from 1,000 patient
resamples within disease, shared across models, outcomes and horizons. Each
model was trained once, so intervals omit training-run variability.

Raw cell effects were reported alongside hierarchical summaries with crossed
disease and outcome effects and a smooth association with training-event
support. Hierarchical summaries weight diseases equally and endpoints equally
within disease; arithmetic summaries weight evaluable cells equally. Support
associations do not estimate the effect of removing labels. Estimability,
priors, practical-effect criteria, decision thresholds and subgroup analyses
are specified in the Supplementary Methods.

Integrated gradients \citep{sundararajan2017ig} described model reliance by
source, time and clinical finding and for individual histories, relative to a
reference preserving the timing of care with average recorded events.
Event-removal and randomisation checks assessed faithfulness
\citep{adebayo2018sanity}. Restricted-input evaluations assessed reliance
without retraining. Full attribution methods and checks are supplementary.
Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI \citep{collins2024tripodai}.

\section*{Discussion}
One shared prediction model, developed from the linked records of patients with
lymphoid cancer, predicted 69 outcomes in 24 subtypes and was compared with the tools
that would otherwise be built for each of them: gradient-boosted trees tuned for
every disease, outcome and horizon, specialists trained on each prediction
alone, and the prognostic indices in their own populations and on their own
endpoints. \placeholder{Principal findings with uncertainty: how the joint model
compared with XGBoost and the IPI, what disease pooling, outcome pooling and
their combination added and whether the two axes were complementary, where the
gains were largest, what national pretraining and local adaptation added, and
the most informative null or harmful result.} These results come from a single
evaluation of patients treated after every modelling decision had been fixed,
while first-line treatment and the registers were changing, so they describe
performance in a later treatment period rather than performance on the data
used to choose the model. Prospective clinical use remains to be evaluated.

The first implication concerns how clinical predictors are built. Risk
prediction in haematology has been developed one disease and one endpoint at a
time, which leaves most complications and rare subtypes without a tool, because
no single problem has enough events. \placeholder{If the results hold: our
findings show that this is not necessary.} Where pooling helps most for the
combinations with fewest events, its main consequence is coverage: estimates
for subtypes and complications for which no dedicated model could be trained or
validated, including subtypes without a routinely used index. These are the
patients for whom clinicians have least quantitative support today. The
important distinction is between making a prediction possible and making it
reliable enough to act on. Shared training may make a model estimable where a
specialist cannot be fitted, but independent evidence for that rare prediction
can remain scarce. Its immediate value may therefore be to identify promising
clinical questions for validation, rather than to justify routine use of every
output. One model is
also one pipeline to validate, monitor and update instead of
\result{narrow_fits}{N}, although a data-quality error in a shared pipeline
affects many predictions at once and each use still needs its own definition
and validation. Sharing does not make diseases interchangeable, and rare
combinations must be judged on their own calibration and uncertainty rather than
on a panel average dominated by common diseases.

The controlled comparisons distinguish the value of additional patients from
that of additional labelled outcomes. This matters because rarity alone does
not guarantee useful transfer: another disease may supply relevant examples of
a complication, whereas another outcome may help the encoder recognise the
patient's underlying vulnerability. The two sources can complement one another,
repeat the same information or conflict. Identifying which pattern occurs is
more informative than concluding that a larger joint model is better on
average. \placeholder{Whether gains came from
other diseases, other outcomes or both, and whether their effects were
complementary.} The attribution analyses address a different question: whether
the fitted shared model uses the history differently for different
complications (\cref{fig:attribution-main}). \placeholder{Outcome-specific
patterns and their agreement with clinical expectations.} Such patterns can
make shared predictions more interpretable, but do not establish the mechanism
of a performance gain. Multi-task studies of health records have reported uneven benefits and
negative transfer \citep{harutyunyan2019multitask,mcdermott2020mtl,ding2019phenotyping}.

The work also changes the starting point for subsequent prediction studies.
The conversion of linked records into longitudinal inputs, reconstruction of
treatment and outcomes, and matched training and evaluation workflows can be
reused when addressing another clinical question. Within the available data,
a new outcome or patient population can build on this foundation rather than
require an entirely separate modelling pipeline. The clinical definition,
eligibility and validation remain specific to the new question, but much of
the technical work is already in place. This is a contribution beyond the
performance of any one model: it makes a broader programme of clinical
prediction research easier to pursue. Nation-scale pretraining could extend
that reuse from the research workflow to the learned representation itself.

A second implication concerns the role of nation-scale pretraining. The
important question is whether a population encoder can be reused for a
specialist service, including outcomes for which it was never explicitly
trained. \placeholder{Whether direct transfer worked, how much local adaptation
added, and whether fewer labelled patients sufficed.} If demonstrated, this
would support a broader approach to clinical prediction in Denmark: a
foundation model learned from population records could become shared
infrastructure on which individual specialties build their prediction models.
The investment in learning a representation of longitudinal health records
could then support many applications, with local adaptation and labelled
outcomes supplying the clinical specificity. A reduction in label requirements
would make this particularly relevant to rare diseases and complications for
which a dedicated cohort is difficult to assemble. Haematology provides a
demanding test of this approach, spanning many malignancies and outcomes and
comparing the resulting predictions with established indices and tuned tabular
models. A benefit here would justify evaluating the same strategy in other
Danish specialties; it would not establish that every clinical task benefits.
Local adaptation would be part of the transfer strategy. Conversely, a benefit
restricted to a few endpoints would limit the case for broad reuse.
These comparisons evaluate the delivered national encoder and its adaptation;
they do not isolate population scale from differences in coding, architecture
or pretraining budget, or establish transfer to another health system.

For patients, the consequence of accurate shared models is a profile of risks at
the start of treatment rather than a single prognostic summary. Disease control
and complications need not have the same predictors or the same responses: a
patient at low risk of treatment failure may be at high risk of serious
infection, and prophylaxis, monitoring and dose planning differ. A profile is
useful only if the probabilities are accurate, which is why we report
calibration alongside discrimination, and it is more useful if a clinician can
see what each risk rests on. Because the model reads the history differently
for each outcome, one patient's explanation can show that a high risk of serious
infection follows a recent pneumonia while a high risk of death follows the
findings of the diagnostic work-up (\cref{fig:attribution-patient}), so that a
prediction can be checked against what the clinician knows of the patient. Such
explanations describe the model rather than causes, and their value for
decisions needs testing with clinicians before use
\citep{ghassemi2021explainability}. The comparison with the IPI connects these
predictions to current practice, because the indices already shape treatment
planning and trial eligibility \citep{DLG2025DLBCL,Tilly2022POLARIX}. Better
separation of outcomes than the IPI in its own population, for example by
recognising fit older patients as being at low risk, justifies evaluating
whether the additional information improves supportive-care decisions. It does
not justify replacing IPI-based treatment rules. An index applies only where it
was recorded or can be computed, often a minority of patients in routine data,
whereas the model gives an estimate for everyone with a record. In both cases the
model predicts risk under the care patients received, not the benefit of
changing it \citep{vanGeloven2020}. Choosing between regimens would require
predictions conditional on the regimen, and whether better stratification leads
to better decisions depends on the thresholds at which decisions are taken,
which a net-benefit analysis can make explicit once the action attached to each
risk is defined \citep{vickers2006dca}.

These results also bear on foundation models for health records, which are
mostly evaluated on a sparse set of tasks such as mortality, heart failure and
long length of stay that say little about their usefulness to health systems
\citep{wornow2023shaky}. Here the same kind of model was evaluated on clinically
defined outcomes with competing deaths, against the indices in guidelines and
against tuned gradient-boosted trees on aligned register sources. The tabular
and sequence pipelines differ in history windows, aggregation and
representation as well as model class, so their comparison estimates the value
of complete pipelines rather than isolating a transformer effect. Differences
that look small as an average AUC can matter for individual patients: at a fixed
alert rate, a small gain in ranking moves some of the patients who will die
early or have a serious infection or severe cytopenia into the group that
receives attention, which is why we report the per-1,000 and decision-point
numbers. Pretrained representations have reduced the labels needed for benchmark
tasks \citep{wornow2023ehrshot}. \placeholder{Whether the national encoder helped
directly, only after local adaptation, or not at all, and the share of training
patients with which it matched the local encoder.} A benefit with fewer patients
would place the value of population pretraining where cohorts are small: rare
subtypes, smaller health systems and new outcomes. The national and local
encoders may differ in population, coding and training budget, so these
comparisons concern the encoders as delivered, not scale alone.

The test period differed from the training period in more than calendar time.
First-line treatment changed \placeholder{confirm for Denmark: e.g. polatuzumab
vedotin in DLBCL, targeted agents in CLL, daratumumab combinations in myeloma}
\citep{Tilly2022POLARIX}, the national patient register moved from LPR2 to LPR3
on 2 February 2019, and laboratory coverage expanded in eastern Denmark from 2016
\citep{brieghel2025dalycare}. Shifts of this kind are a common cause of failure
for clinical prediction models \citep{finlayson2021shift}. \placeholder{If
performance held, this supports robustness over the observed period}, although
not to future changes or to another health system, and a deployed model needs
monitoring and periodic updating \citep{davis2017drift}, simpler for one model
than for a portfolio. Because the registers are national, one model could serve
every hospital, provided it works equally well in every region, which it
\placeholder{did / did not} (\cref{fig:supp-regions}). Deployment of this kind
has precedent: CLL-TIM was deployed alongside the electronic health record of
eastern Denmark and validated in patients diagnosed after its development
\citep{Agius2024clltim}. A deployed model would need its inputs at the time of
the decision, from hospital systems rather than registers updated with a delay,
in the same MEDS format, with performance confirmed on data as they are then
available, and the appropriate regulatory assessment. Beyond accuracy, a
sequence model gives every patient one learned representation, so all the risks
shown for a patient come from one consistent reading of the record
(\cref{fig:patient-map}). Neighbourhoods in this representation can suggest
where predictions need closer investigation, although they are not validated
measures of clinical similarity or uncertainty
(\cref{fig:supp-calibration-maps,fig:supp-embedding-comparison,fig:supp-embedding-gallery-1,fig:supp-embedding-gallery-2,fig:supp-embedding-gallery-3,fig:supp-embedding-gallery-4,fig:supp-embedding-gallery-5,fig:supp-embedding-gallery-6}).
The atlas of predictable outcomes (\cref{fig:atlas}) is informative whichever
model performs best: it shows which risks can be anticipated at the start of
treatment and where data remain insufficient, and we report weak and harmful
results alongside the favourable ones for this reason.

This study has limitations. Predictions estimate risk under observed care and do
not show that acting on them improves outcomes, which requires defined actions,
decision analysis and prospective evaluation. Validation was temporal within one
health system, not geographic, and some test patients contributed earlier
history to self-supervised pretraining, so the evaluation concerns later
treatment episodes rather than unseen individuals. Another country would need
input harmonisation and possibly adaptation, but the workflow itself is
portable: the conversion to MEDS, the training protocol and the evaluation let
another health system train and validate its own model. Outcomes depend on
ascertainment, follow-up and competing death, laboratory thresholds and
treatment-based proxies are not complete clinical assessments of adverse
events, some subtypes are coded after treatment starts, and laboratory risk sets
condition on early monitoring, which censoring weights do not remove. Training
support varied naturally across combinations, so its relation to the benefit of
sharing is an association, not the effect of removing labels. Each model was
trained once, so intervals omit variation between training runs, and all
selection used a tuning year of 1,980 patients, small for the rarest subtypes,
which we addressed by keeping the choices few and prespecified. For most indices
the registers record the risk category rather than the score, which limits those
comparisons to stratification and discrimination. The sequence models did not
read the quality-registry fields for performance status, stage, diagnostic LDH
or cytogenetics, although pre-index LDH results appear in their laboratory
histories, and the registry-augmented XGBoost shows what these fields add.
Attributions describe the fitted model relative to a reference and can move
between correlated events, so agreement with clinical expectations shows
plausibility, which the removal and randomisation checks complement
\citep{sundararajan2017ig,adebayo2018sanity}. The many correlated comparisons
share patient resamples and were summarised hierarchically, but the raw paired
estimates remain the primary evidence.

\placeholder{Conclusion. Draft if the results hold: clinical risk prediction in
haematology does not have to be built one disease and one outcome at a time. A
shared model can combine labelled experience across diseases and outcomes,
while nation-scale pretraining can extend its starting knowledge beyond the
specialist cohort. Where these gains are demonstrated against tuned tabular
models and established indices, they support a different approach to prediction:
a reusable representation adapted to many clinical questions, including those
too small to sustain a dedicated model. The extent of that benefit, its limits
and its effect on clinical decisions must be judged separately. Its value at
the bedside now needs prospective evaluation.}

\begin{abstract}
Clinical prediction in haematology is commonly organized as a collection of
isolated problems: a disease-specific score or model is trained for one cohort,
one endpoint, and one prediction horizon. Borrowing labelled information across
related diseases is known to help, and pretraining a sequence model across an
entire population is the limiting form of that idea; whether this so-called
foundation-model approach delivers on its promise where labelled data are
genuinely scarce is unresolved. Using a nationwide
linked resource covering Danish adults diagnosed with lymphoid cancers, we
assembled a longitudinal prediction cohort spanning 24 haematological disease
cohorts and 87 clinical outcomes, and evaluated prediction at 30, 60, 90, 180,
365, and 730 days. We
compared established diagnostic risk scores, task-specific gradient-boosted
trees, locally pretrained sequence models, and a large-scale electronic health
record foundation model. We then tested whether a shared longitudinal encoder
could borrow information across disease cohorts and outcome tasks using
multi-outcome time-to-event learning, structured prediction heads, and
cohort-specific specialist adaptation. \placeholder{Insert locked headline
results: foundation versus local pretraining; transformer versus XGBoost;
joint versus cohort-specific training; benefit in rare cohorts.} These results
define when scale, temporal resolution, cohort relatedness, and task relatedness
provide useful information for clinical prediction, and provide a systematic
map of predictability across haematological care.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent\textbf{Editorial summary}\quad
\textit{Borrowing information across related diseases is an established way to
improve clinical prediction, and population-scale pretraining is its most
ambitious form. This study asks whether that approach delivers where labelled
data are genuinely scarce, and which forms of sharing across diseases and
outcomes are responsible for any benefit.}

\section*{Introduction}
Anticipating a patient's future state is one of the central tasks of clinical
medicine. In haematology, prognosis determines whether a patient receives
intensive or attenuated therapy, whether transplantation or maintenance is
considered, which clinical trials they are eligible for and which trials are
enriched for the patients most likely to benefit, and which prophylactic and
monitoring precautions are put in place. A prognostic estimate is therefore not
an academic quantity: it is an input to decisions that change what treatment a
patient receives and what harms they are protected against. Improving these
estimates, and knowing which of them can be improved at all, has direct
clinical consequences.

Risk stratification in haematology has traditionally begun with concise
clinical scores measured near diagnosis, such as the NCCN International
Prognostic Index in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma or the CLL-IPI in chronic
lymphocytic leukaemia \citep{zhou2014nccnipi,cllipi2016}. Such scores are
transparent and portable, but compress a patient's state into a small number of
variables and are ordinarily developed for one disease and one endpoint.
Machine learning has since been applied across haematological malignancy
\citep{radakovich2020ml}, typically by aggregating diagnoses, laboratory
values, procedures, and medication histories into a tabular representation;
work in chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, for example, has used this approach to
predict infection and treatment need from routinely collected data
\citep{agius2020clltim}. Wider feature sets can improve discrimination, but the
aggregation discards event order, and models are still usually fitted
separately for every cohort--outcome--horizon combination
(\cref{fig:paradigms}).

That labelled data can be borrowed across related diseases is not a new idea,
and it is already established. Multi-task learning trains one model on several
clinical endpoints so that each benefits from the others
\citep{harutyunyan2019multitask}, and meta-learning formalizes the same
intuition by learning across a set of related risk-prediction tasks in order to
predict a target task for which few labels exist
\citep{finn2017maml,zhang2019metapred}. MetaPred in particular showed that a
predictor for a low-resource clinical risk can be improved by training across
other, better-resourced risks rather than on the target alone
\citep{zhang2019metapred}. The principle that motivates this study is therefore
not in question; what remains open is how far it extends, and in what form it
is best realized.

Transformer models are the modern instantiation of that same principle.
They operate directly on ordered sequences of coded events, can retain repeated
measurements and long-range temporal context, and can be pretrained without
outcome labels \citep{li2020behrt,rasmy2021med,pang2021cehr,odgaard2024core}.
Pretraining on a broad patient population is cohort breadth taken to its limit:
instead of selecting a handful of related tasks, the model is fitted to every
disease and every recorded event in a health system, and the target task is
reached by fine-tuning. This has been scaled in several directions. Predictive
models have been built directly on raw longitudinal records rather than curated
variables \citep{rajkomar2018scalable}; pretraining objectives have been
extended from masked tokens to predicting all of a patient's future diagnoses
at once \citep{yang2023transformehr}; models have been trained at health-system
scale and deployed prospectively \citep{jiang2023nyutron}; and national
registry sequences have been used to predict cancer risk from disease
trajectories and to model life trajectories as sequences of events
\citep{placido2023pancreatic,savcisens2024life2vec}. The last two are
particularly relevant here, since both operate on Danish nationwide registry
data and establish that sequence models are informative in this data
environment. Closest to the present work, time-to-event pretraining across many
outcomes has been shown to improve label efficiency and robustness to temporal
shift \citep{steinberg2023motor}. The limiting case, and the one this study is
positioned against, is a model pretrained across all diseases in an entire
national population.

Whether that promise holds in practice is one of the central questions of this
study. Foundation models are frequently presented as a general solution to label
scarcity, which would make them exactly the right tool for rare haematological
diseases and sparse endpoints. The supporting evidence is more equivocal than
the framing suggests. Critical reviews find that most clinical foundation
models are trained and evaluated on narrow datasets and tasks that reveal
little about their usefulness to health systems \citep{wornow2023shaky};
benchmarks have been built specifically because few-shot benefit could not
otherwise be measured \citep{wornow2023ehrshot}; compact domain-specific models
have been found to match or exceed much larger general-purpose ones
\citep{lehman2023clinical}; and on tabular data of the size typical of a single
clinical cohort, gradient-boosted trees remain difficult to beat
\citep{grinsztajn2022tabular}. A study of a specialized registry is an
informative place to ask the question, because it contains precisely the
low-resource cells where the promise should matter most.

Specialized registries also expose a second form of structure that this
literature has largely left unexamined. Alongside related patient populations
there are related clinical outcomes: a sparse endpoint may be statistically
connected to laboratory abnormalities, treatment escalation, infections, or
survival. Existing multi-outcome models generally treat the outcome panel as a
convenience for pretraining or as a set of independent heads, rather than
asking which diseases and which endpoints actually inform one another.

Our own previous work in this setting motivates that question directly. We
first assembled a nationwide linked resource for lymphoid cancers
\citep{brieghel2025dalycare}, and then used it to develop gradient-boosted
models of treatment failure after first-line therapy in diffuse large B-cell
lymphoma, which improved on established prognostic indices and reduced
age-driven misclassification \citep{werling2026dlbcl}. Two aspects of that
study, rather than its headline performance, motivate the present one. First,
training across all aggressive lymphoma subtypes performed about as well on
diffuse large B-cell lymphoma as restricting training to that diagnosis,
suggesting that prognostic structure is partly shared between related diseases.
This is consistent with the meta-learning results above, although it was
obtained with an aggregated tabular model and without that framing. The
observation was incidental to the aim of the study, concerned a single endpoint
within one closely related group of lymphomas, and was never tested as a
hypothesis in its own right. Second, constructing the feature table required
aggregating each history into fixed lookback windows, discarding the order and
spacing of clinical events; we identified this as the principal remaining
source of unused information. Whether cross-disease transfer extends across the
full range of haematological diagnoses, whether it holds for endpoints other
than treatment failure, and whether related outcomes support one another in the
same way, are questions a single-disease study cannot answer.

We therefore frame clinical information along five axes. The conventional
axes are the number of patients, the number of measured variables, and the
number of longitudinal observations. To these we add cohort breadth and task
breadth: the extent to which a target predictor gains inductive information
from related diseases and outcomes without treating them as identical
(\cref{fig:information}). Neither added axis is novel in itself, and both are
implicit in the meta-learning and pretraining literature above. What has been
missing is a setting in which they can be varied deliberately and measured
against each other, and against the conventional axes, on the same patients and
the same endpoints.

This framing yields three central questions. First, does the promise of
large-scale pretraining hold here: does a general-population foundation model
outperform local haematology pretraining and task-specific tabular models, and
is any advantage concentrated where labelled data are scarce? Second, can joint
training across diseases reduce the penalty associated with rare cohorts and
limited labels, as the meta-learning literature would predict? Third, which
forms of cross-outcome sharing improve prediction, and which merely move
performance between endpoints?

To answer these questions we used DALY-CARE, a nationwide linked resource that
integrates ten Danish national registers with hospital electronic health
records, laboratory results, and disease-specific quality registries for adults
diagnosed with lymphoid cancers since 2002 \citep{brieghel2025dalycare}. The
resource description reported the cohort available at the time of that report;
the present study draws a later extract, so the denominators here are not
expected to match those figures. Our study population is also defined
differently from our earlier lymphoma analysis. That study indexed at the start
of first-line therapy in aggressive lymphoma and asked one question at one
horizon, whereas the present study indexes first-line treatment episodes across
the full haematological spectrum and evaluates many endpoints at several
horizons. The two populations overlap substantially and share a common data
substrate, but they are not identical and the results are not directly
comparable patient for patient.

On this basis we defined a prospective temporal evaluation with held-out
patients indexed from 2023 onward, and evaluated 87 outcomes
across 24 fine disease cohorts and six horizons. We compared clinical scores,
EHR-only and registry-augmented XGBoost models, multiple pretraining scopes,
joint and cohort-specific neural models, and structured multi-outcome readouts.
The study is designed both as a model comparison and as an empirical atlas of
what can be predicted from routinely collected longitudinal data.

\section*{Discussion}
This study evaluates a proposition that extends beyond a single architecture:
clinical information can be increased not only by observing more patients,
variables, or time points, but also by structuring how related diseases and
outcomes contribute to learning. The linked haematology setting is unusually
suited to this question because it combines shared care pathways with marked
variation in disease frequency, treatment, follow-up, and endpoint support.

The underlying idea is not new. Multi-task and meta-learning approaches
established that a low-resource clinical prediction task can be improved by
training across related, better-resourced tasks
\citep{harutyunyan2019multitask,zhang2019metapred}, and population-scale
pretraining is the most ambitious expression of that idea rather than a
departure from it. What is contested is whether the ambitious version pays for
itself, and in particular whether it resolves the label scarcity that makes
rare diseases difficult in the first place. Our results speak to that question
in a setting where scarcity is structural rather than simulated: cohort sizes
and event counts vary by orders of magnitude within a single linked resource
and a single care system, so the low-resource cells are real clinical
populations rather than subsampled ones.

\placeholder{Insert concise interpretation of the four principal results.}
Several outcomes may remain better served by task-specific gradient boosting,
particularly when a compact set of recent measurements dominates risk. Such a
finding would not invalidate the foundation-model approach; it identifies the
conditions under which representation learning adds value and prevents model
complexity from being treated as an end in itself.

This comparison is not made against a convenient baseline. The tabular
comparator is built by the same feature-construction pipeline as our earlier
lymphoma models, which improved on established prognostic indices in this
setting \citep{werling2026dlbcl}, so the two model classes see the same source
data assembled in the same way. What the present study removes are the two
constraints that approach imposed: the aggregation of each history into fixed
lookback windows, and the fitting of a separate model for every
cohort--outcome--horizon cell. Interpreted this way, the results describe not
whether machine learning helps in haematology, which we and others have already
shown \citep{radakovich2020ml,agius2020clltim}, but whether temporal resolution
and structured sharing across diseases and endpoints add information beyond a
strong task-specific tabular model. Our earlier observation that training
across aggressive lymphoma subtypes did not degrade diffuse large B-cell
lymphoma performance is here promoted from an incidental finding to a
prespecified estimand, evaluated across the full disease spectrum and the
complete endpoint panel rather than a single cohort and endpoint.

The study also differs from existing clinical foundation-model work in what it
varies. Prior time-to-event pretraining has demonstrated that many-outcome
objectives improve label efficiency \citep{steinberg2023motor}, and few-shot
benchmarks have been built to measure adaptation under label scarcity
\citep{wornow2023ehrshot}, but both hold the downstream task structure fixed
and vary the encoder. Here the encoder is held fixed and the sharing structure
is varied, which addresses the concern that clinical foundation models are
often evaluated in ways that do not identify where their benefit originates
\citep{wornow2023shaky}. Reporting the full outcome panel, including cells that
are not estimable, is intended to serve the same end.

The study also separates three often conflated decisions: how outcomes share
model capacity, how noisy tasks are weighted during optimization, and how model
performance is averaged for selection. Family definitions used for clinical
reporting need not determine loss weights or neural trunks. Our confirmatory
analysis therefore treats task reliability and task grouping as explicit,
auditable components.

Limitations include the single national healthcare context, residual changes
in coding and measurement practices, variation in registry coverage, and the
possibility that temporal transport from 2021 to 2023+ reflects both disease
and healthcare-system drift. Because the cohort definition, index date, and
data extract differ from our earlier lymphoma analysis, the two studies cannot
be compared directly, and any apparent change in performance for overlapping
endpoints reflects these design differences as well as the change in model
class. The large endpoint panel also creates multiplicity
and correlated estimands. We address these issues using prospective temporal
splits, outcome-specific eligibility, paired patient-level comparisons,
hierarchical uncertainty models, and complete reporting of the prediction
atlas rather than selective endpoint publication.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Data sources and study population}
All data were obtained from DALY-CARE, which links ten Danish national
registers with hospital electronic health records, laboratory results,
pathology, microbiology, and disease-specific quality registries for adults
diagnosed with lymphoid cancers since 2002 \citep{brieghel2025dalycare}. The
resource publication describes the data available at the time of that report;
the present study uses a later extract with additional accrued follow-up, and
cohort counts therefore differ from the published resource description.
\placeholder{Insert approved descriptions of the specific linked EHR sources,
RKKP/quality data, extract date, ethical approvals, data governance, and
linkage keys.}

The index date is the start of a reconstructed first-line treatment episode.
This differs from our earlier lymphoma study, which indexed first-line therapy
in aggressive lymphoma only and required registry-confirmed treatment
\citep{werling2026dlbcl}; the present cohort spans all included haematological
diseases and admits episodes reconstructed from treatment records where
registry confirmation is unavailable (\cref{fig:cohort-flow}). Patient overlap
between the two studies is therefore substantial but partial, and no result
reported here should be read as a direct replication of that analysis.

Patients entered a supervised prediction cohort only when registry eligibility
and the outcome-specific risk-set definition were satisfied at index time.
Self-supervised pretraining could additionally include patients with eligible
pre-index longitudinal records, and its denominator is reported separately.

\subsection*{Temporal study design}
All splits were defined at the patient level using a shared index date. Training
included index dates through 31 December 2021, model selection used index dates
from 1 January through 31 December 2022, and held-out evaluation began on 1
January 2023. Held-out labels were not used for architecture, checkpoint, task
weight, or hyperparameter selection. Patient histories were truncated at the
prediction time. During masked-language-model pretraining, long histories were
expanded into non-overlapping context windows so that earlier history remained
available without future leakage.

\subsection*{Cohorts, outcomes, and competing events}
The canonical inventory contained 24 fine diagnoses nested within 10 grouped
cohorts. Outcomes were represented as time to primary event, time to competing
death where applicable, or censoring. Outcomes already containing death as
part of their definition were modelled as one-cause endpoints. Prediction was
evaluated at 30, 60, 90, 180, 365, and 730 days. Full definitions, code lists,
eligibility rules, and cohort-flow counts are provided in
\placeholder{Supplementary Tables}.

\subsection*{Sequence representation and pretraining}
Longitudinal records were converted to ordered sequences of diagnoses,
procedures, medications, laboratory concepts, encounter descriptors, and
background tokens. Numerical measurements were represented using prespecified
bins. The encoder used a causal four-layer transformer with Fourier absolute
time encoding and a maximum context of 8,192 tokens in the local experiments.
Masked-language-model pretraining was evaluated at prespecified milestones to
separate training duration from population scale. \placeholder{Insert final
10-million-patient encoder specification when delivered.}

\subsection*{Multi-outcome prediction}
The direct model produced outcome- and interval-specific primary-event and
competing-event hazards, following the discrete-time and competing-risk
formulations established for neural survival models
\citep{gensheimer2019nnetsurvival,lee2018deephit} and extended here to many
outcomes in a single pass \citep{steinberg2023motor}. Piecewise-constant
hazards were transformed into monotone cumulative incidence functions at each
evaluation horizon. We compared exact-time competing-risk likelihood with a
censoring-aware multi-horizon binary cross-entropy objective. Where outcomes
were weighted by learned task uncertainty, we used homoscedastic uncertainty
weighting \citep{kendall2018multitask}. A curriculum introduced tasks according
to outcome support. Alternative readouts are summarized in
\cref{fig:architectures}.

\subsection*{Specialist fine-tuning}
After selecting one joint architecture using tuning data, we initialized one
specialist per fine cohort and outcome from the complete parent prediction
path. Each specialist retained all six horizons and was fine-tuned end to end.
For factorized cohort--outcome attention, fine diagnoses inherited the routing
of their prespecified grouped parent before specialization. This stage tests
whether joint learning is most useful as a final predictor or as post-training
initialization for a local specialist.

\subsection*{Comparator models}
Clinical scores were evaluated without refitting where their required inputs
were available. XGBoost models were trained separately for each cohort,
outcome, and horizon using locked EHR-derived feature matrices. These feature
matrices were produced by the same feature-construction pipeline used in our
earlier lymphoma study, which aggregates each data modality over fixed lookback
windows before the index date \citep{werling2026dlbcl}; only the cohort,
endpoint, and horizon definitions differ. The tabular comparator is therefore
the same modelling approach applied to the present task inventory, not a
reimplementation, and the contrast with the sequence model isolates temporal
representation and cross-task sharing rather than differences in feature
engineering. Registry-augmented tabular models were restricted to patients with
registry quality variables available at prediction time. Hyperparameters were
selected using the 2022 tuning split and all comparisons used identical
held-out subjects.

\subsection*{Model selection and statistical analysis}
We distinguish checkpoint selection, architecture selection, and held-out
reporting. Checkpoints were selected using tuning data only. The confirmatory
architecture score first averages horizons within an outcome and then averages
outcomes, preventing outcomes with more estimable horizons from receiving
greater weight. Population-weighted and equal-cohort summaries are reported as
complementary estimands.

Held-out model differences are calculated from paired patient predictions.
Bootstrap intervals resample patients within the evaluation population. A
hierarchical Bayesian model estimates the overall model contrast and variation
by cohort, outcome, horizon, clinical domain, and training support. We report
posterior probabilities of superiority and practical equivalence using a
prespecified AUC region of practical equivalence
\placeholder{lock threshold, proposed $\pm0.005$}. Discrimination is reported
using cumulative/dynamic AUC; probabilistic accuracy and calibration are
reported using Brier score, calibration intercept, and calibration slope.

\subsection*{A common basis for comparing predictions}
The unit of clinical evaluation is a disease--outcome--horizon combination.
Its risk set is determined by endpoint eligibility and ascertainment, not by
which model performs best. For each split we report eligible patients, outcome
events, competing deaths and patients whose status is unknown at the horizon.
A paired comparison then uses patients with predictions from both methods.
Different contrasts may have different common risk sets; their denominators
are reported rather than hidden by a single panel-wide total. A model that
cannot be fitted or a metric that cannot be estimated remains in the coverage
inventory with its reason. Such entries are not zero effects.

The study distinguishes superiority among evaluable targets from coverage of
additional targets. A pooled model can produce predictions where a specialist
cannot be fitted, but there is then no observed specialist performance to
subtract. Those targets receive absolute performance estimates and support
information. They do not enter a paired superiority average through imputation.
The full outcome definitions and treatment reconstruction are described in
the appended data-definition supplement.

\subsection*{Clinical scores on the questions they were developed to answer}
A prognostic index assigns a score from selected clinical measurements and
uses it to rank patients or assign risk categories. Its interpretation depends
on the original disease population, clinical time point and endpoint. We
therefore distinguish an intended-endpoint benchmark from an extension to a
new endpoint. The former compares the score, logistic regression, XGBoost and
sequence models for the score's original target in its applicable population.
The latter asks whether that same score carries information about another
risk, such as a treatment complication; it is reported as a separate question.
Original and evaluated endpoints, horizons and prediction times are recorded
side by side. A shorter available follow-up horizon is not described as a
reproduction of a longer-horizon development result.

All methods in a score comparison use identical score-available patients.
Score availability is established at the prediction origin, and its frequency
is reported by disease and calendar period. Original score categories remain
unchanged. A tabular comparator restricted to score components examines what
can be learned from the same clinical snapshot. Richer-history tabular and
sequence models examine the additional value of information in routine
histories. A gain in the latter comparison is a gain for the delivered
pipeline; it does not isolate neural architecture from information content.

Integer score values are not individual probabilities. They can be used
directly for ranking-based discrimination, whereas Brier scores and probability
calibration require an applicable published probability mapping or a mapping
fitted on development data and frozen before testing. That mapping's data,
endpoint, horizon and fitting procedure accompany the reported estimates.
The full comparison roster includes unsupported and unavailable scores.

\subsection*{Risk groups, age and operating points}
Continuous model risks are converted to baseline groups using cutoffs selected
on tuning data. The clinical-score groups retain their original definitions.
Kaplan--Meier curves describe death-inclusive endpoints; Aalen--Johansen
cumulative incidence describes nonfatal endpoints with competing death.
Group sizes, events, follow-up support and uncertainty accompany the curves.
Group membership is fixed at baseline and does not change as follow-up proceeds.
Patients without an applicable score are not silently added to a paired score
comparison or assigned to a favourable group.

Age displays separate the number of patients from predicted and observed
probabilities. The same age bands and group definitions underlie each row.
The prediction row shows mean sequence-model probability, including within
clinical-score categories; it is not a probability supplied by the index.
The observed row uses the appropriate survival or competing-risk estimator.
Sparse-cell counts remain visible when probability estimates are suppressed.
These descriptive point summaries alone cannot establish an age effect or a
calibration difference. Uncertainty and ascertainment must support any such claim.

Operating-point comparisons use thresholds locked on tuning data. Matching
specificity during tuning does not guarantee identical specificity in the
test period. Report achieved sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative
predictive values and uncertainty, together with the actual patient counts.
Known-status confusion counts exclude patients censored before the horizon;
those exclusions are reported. Censoring-weighted quantities are labelled as
weighted estimates and are not presented as literal patient counts.

\subsection*{Censoring, calibration and robustness}
Known-status and censoring-weighted evaluation answer different questions and
are kept separate in figures and tables. For inverse-probability weighting,
report the censoring model, its fitting population, censoring survival at each
horizon, weight distribution and the fraction affected by any numerical floor.
Weak follow-up support can make a long-horizon estimate unstable even when the
underlying prediction table is large. Sensitivity to censoring assumptions and
weighting therefore accompanies substantive claims at those horizons.

Calibration reporting includes calibration-in-the-large, slope where estimable
and reliability examples chosen under a documented rule. A discrimination gain
does not imply more accurate absolute probabilities. No test-period
recalibration is part of the primary comparison. Any separately evaluated
calibration procedure must be fitted on development data and identified as a
new pipeline variant. Horizon, calendar, patient-history and diagnosis-timing
sensitivities report their own target populations. Raw paired effects accompany hierarchical summaries; the support
spline, unsmoothed and binned analyses are distinguished rather than interpreted
as independent replications.

\subsection*{Patient representations and training provenance}
The patient embedding comparison uses the same patients at five stages:
local pretraining, tuning-selected local fine-tuning, PHAIR pretraining,
local adaptation of PHAIR, and tuning-selected PHAIR fine-tuning. The final
stage identifies whether fine-tuning began directly from PHAIR or from its
adapted export. Record checkpoint hashes, extraction layer, patient pooling,
input cutoff, context truncation, preprocessing and projection parameters.
Each model's projection is fitted on tuning-set representations and applied
unchanged to test representations. Within a model, coordinates are reused when colouring
by disease, score group, age or sequence length. Sequence length denotes the
tokens entering the encoder; pre-truncation history length is a separate field.
Scores outside their intended population remain unavailable.

Coordinates from separately fitted projections have no common metric scale.
Apparent clusters or changes in separation are descriptive and do not establish
prediction superiority, biological mechanisms or the effect of transfer.
Individual-point outputs require the applicable secure-data disclosure review.
Vocabulary maps, if reported, distinguish input-token embeddings from contextual
patient representations and record vocabulary alignment and token frequency.
Actual model configuration and training exposure are described in the appended
model-development supplement; planned epoch budgets are not completed-run data.

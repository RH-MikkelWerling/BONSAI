\operasubsection{Data sources and processing}
All data were held and processed in the DALY-CARE environment at the Danish
National Genome Center \citep{brieghel2025dalycare}. Registers were linked
through the civil registration number and converted to the Medical Event Data
Standard (MEDS) with ehr2meds (\url{https://github.com/FGA-DIKU/ehr2meds},
configurations \texttt{preMEDS/lymphoma\_ngc.yaml},
\texttt{MEDS/lymphoma\_ngc\_event.yaml} and \texttt{MEDS/lymphoma\_pipeline.yaml},
main branch, commit \texttt{5562abc}). MEDS stores every record as an event
with a patient, a time, a code and an optional value, so all sources share one
format. The conversion has three steps, described below, followed by
tokenisation in BONSAI. \Cref{tab:meds-sources} lists the sources, what each
contributes and when its events are dated, and \cref{fig:model-inputs} shows
how they form one sequence per patient.

The registers arrive as separate tables, each with its own identifiers, column
names and conventions for time, and the first task of the conversion is to give
them a common form. Personal identifiers were replaced by integer subject
identifiers, and timestamps were assembled from the separate date, hour and
minute fields in which the registers store them. Diagnoses and procedures are
recorded against a hospital contact rather than a patient, so they were joined
to their contact, which supplies the patient and the contact's start and end.
Contacts, pathology requisitions and hospital medication orders were then
assigned a region and a primary specialty from the national register of
health-care organisations (SOR). Because departments merge, move and change
specialty over time, each record used the organisation as it was registered on
the date of the record. Records from the older patient register (LPR2), which
predates SOR, took their specialty from the register's own specialty code and
their region from the hospital code.

The change from LPR2 to LPR3 in 2019 also changed how some contact attributes
are coded. Where a documented correspondence exists, LPR2 values were mapped to
their LPR3 equivalents, so that a token means the same thing before and after
the change: acute and planned priority became \texttt{ATA1} and
\texttt{ATA3}, and the reasons for contact became the corresponding
\texttt{ALCC} codes. Values without a documented equivalent were kept as they
were rather than guessed.

Each record then became one or more events, and the time given to an event
determines what the model can see at the start of treatment. Neither patient
register records when during a contact a diagnosis was made, so diagnoses were
placed at the end of their contact, the first moment they are certain to have
been known. A diagnosis made during an admission that spans the start of
treatment is therefore not visible to the model. Attributes known when a
contact opens, such as its reason, priority, type, specialty and region, were
placed at its start, procedures when they were performed, prescriptions at
dispensing, hospital medication at the start of the order and laboratory
results at sampling. Sex was a background event without a time. A laboratory
result was treated as a number only if the whole result parsed as one, so that
\texttt{12.5} is numeric but \texttt{<0.1} is kept as text.

The events were then processed by a MEDS-Transforms pipeline. It first assigned
patients at random to a fitting split (90\%) and a remaining split (10\%). This
split exists only to fit the statistics described below. It is unrelated to
the study's training, tuning and test periods and plays no part in which
patients are evaluated. Exact duplicates were removed, including results whose
numeric and text forms were identical, as were events whose code had an empty
part or was recorded as unknown (\texttt{UNK} or the Danish \texttt{UKENDT},
for example \texttt{DIAGNOSIS//UKENDT}). Events at the same time kept the order
of their source table, so the sequence is reproducible. The last stages shaped
the vocabulary by merging rare codes and binning laboratory values, as
described next, and joined each laboratory result with its bin or its text
value into a single code, \texttt{LABTEST//<NPU code>//BIN\_<k>} or
\texttt{LABTEST//<NPU code>//<text>}.

Rare codes give the model too few examples to learn from, so codes with fewer
than 100 events were merged into the nearest ancestor in their hierarchy that
reached 100 events. Diagnoses could be shortened to 4, 3 and 2 characters,
procedures from 8 down to 2, ATC codes to levels 5, 4, 3 and 1, and SNOMED
pathology codes to 5, 4 and 3 characters. Codes that never reached the
threshold were kept, and laboratory and contact codes were not merged. The
counts were taken over the fitting split and the whole period. What this does
and does not affect is described under ``National encoder: data and
representation''.

Laboratory values were binned for each NPU code separately. Values were clipped
at the 1st and 99th percentiles, scaled and assigned to quantile bins, with the
number of bins growing with the number of distinct values,
$B = \mathrm{round}(1.14\,N_{\mathrm{distinct}}^{0.237})$, bounded between 2
and 100. This is the power law Montgomery et al.\ fitted for the best number of
bins in transformers for health records \citep{montgomery2026numeric}. No unit
conversion was needed, because each NPU code has a fixed unit. The bins were
fitted on events before 2022 from patients in the fitting split and applied to
every event.

Finally, BONSAI \citep{montgomery2025bonsai} turned each patient's events into the sequence the models read
(configuration \texttt{daly\_care\_joined\_bins\_data.yaml}), reading each
joined laboratory code as one token. The vocabulary
was fitted on events before 1 January 2022 from patients in the fitting split,
and codes first seen after that date were read as an unknown token, so no code
that appears only in the test period has a learned embedding. Sex and age opened
the sequence as background tokens, a separator token marked each change of
time, so that events recorded together stay grouped, and every event kept its
time. For each prediction the sequence was cut strictly before the start of
treatment and, if longer than 8,192 tokens, shortened to the background tokens
and the most recent events.

The ehr2meds repository contains the configurations for our cohort and for the
national extract (\texttt{phair\_lymphoma}), together with the SOR mapping and
the code that builds it, so the conversion can be rerun on the same registers
inside the DALY-CARE environment. The paper's own code for training, evaluation
and figures is listed under Code availability.


\operasubsection{Model development}

\paragraph{What the model reads.} Each patient was represented as one
chronological sequence of events from the sources in \cref{tab:meds-sources} and \cref{fig:model-inputs}:
diagnoses, procedures, pathology codes, medications, laboratory results and
the attributes of hospital contacts (type, reason, priority, specialty and
region), preceded by tokens for sex and age. Every event carried its time,
encoded with a scaled Time2Vec representation \citep{kazemi2019time2vec}, and
the sequence was cut strictly before the index date, so the model saw only
what was recorded before treatment started. Laboratory results entered as
tokens that combine the test with its value bin, a hybrid representation that
keeps the value while remaining robust to outliers. The number of bins per test followed the power law that Montgomery et al.\ derived for this purpose
\citep{montgomery2026numeric}. Longer histories were cut to the sex and age
tokens plus the most recent events, up to 8,192 tokens in total.

\paragraph{Encoder and pretraining.} The encoder builds on CORE-BEHRT \citep{odgaard2024core}, a bidirectional model, adapted here to a causal transformer \citep{vaswani2017attention} with
a hidden size of 256, four layers and four attention heads of 64 dimensions,
pretrained to predict each next event from the events before it. Pretraining
used every DALY-CARE patient's history up to 1 January 2022, ran for 20
epochs, and kept the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss.
\placeholder{Number of individuals and events, training time and selected
epoch.} This encoder initialised every supervised model in the main
comparisons.

\paragraph{Prediction head and loss.} Every supervised model consisted of the
pretrained encoder, trained further, and a linear head that produced, for each
outcome, piecewise-constant hazards for the outcome and for competing death
\citep{gensheimer2019nnetsurvival,lee2018deephit}, from which the cumulative
incidence at each horizon was derived. These references motivate survival
and competing-risk neural models; the implemented piecewise-hazard
parameterisation with horizon BCE is not the original DeepHit objective. The loss was a binary cross-entropy at
each horizon: patients with the event before the horizon were positive,
patients who died of another cause or were followed beyond the horizon were
negative, and patients censored earlier did not contribute at that horizon.
This known-status training objective is not a censoring-weighted survival
likelihood: different horizons can be learned from different observed subsets.
Censoring-weighted evaluation and calibration checks assess the resulting
predictions but do not retrospectively change the training objective.
Losses were averaged within each outcome and then across endpoints, where the
grade variants of one laboratory finding (grade $\geq$2 and $\geq$3) and nested
definitions of the same finding formed one endpoint (69 outcomes, 52
endpoints), so that a finding defined at two grades did not count twice. This
weighting is separate from the clinical domains used to present results.

\paragraph{Supervision scopes and training.} The four scopes differed only in
which cells of the disease-by-outcome grid (one outcome in one disease) provided labels (\cref{fig:supervision-grid}): a specialist learned
one outcome in one disease, a disease-pooled model one outcome across all
diseases, an outcome-pooled model all outcomes in one disease, and the joint
model all outcomes in all diseases. Every model predicted all six horizons and
was trained with the same optimiser, an effective batch of 256 patients and the
same sampling settings, for up to 30 epochs, stopping after five epochs without
improvement in tuning loss and keeping the tuning-best checkpoint. Every
comparison used the same target patients, labels and horizons. Because equal
epochs do not mean equal optimisation steps or computation, both are reported
for each scope. Checkpoint selection uses the labels available within the
model's scope; the contrast therefore evaluates adding supervision under this
common selection rule, including its effect on optimisation and checkpoint
selection, rather than holding every optimisation trajectory fixed.

\operasubsection{Outcome panel}
We predicted 69 outcomes covering disease course (second-line treatment,
treatment failure, transformation, new malignancy and death), infection,
haematological toxicity and transfusion, kidney, liver and coagulation
toxicity, electrolyte and metabolic disturbances, cardiovascular events and
acute care, each at 30, 60, 90, 180, 365 and 730 days. Laboratory outcomes used
grading thresholds aligned with, but not identical to, the Common Terminology
Criteria for Adverse Events \citep{nci2025ctcae}, with baseline-aware thresholds
when pre-treatment values were already abnormal. Infection was additionally captured through antimicrobial treatment, which we label as such. A patient
contributed to an outcome only if it was ascertainable for them, for example if laboratory follow-up was available, and was otherwise treated as missing
rather than negative. Death was a competing event for every non-fatal outcome.
Definitions, code lists and risk-set sizes are
given in Supplementary Methods S5--S9.

\operasubsection{Tabular comparators: features and tuning}
For each disease--outcome cell and horizon, we trained gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost) \citep{chen2016xgboost} and ridge-penalised logistic regression on the
same patients, labels and competing-event handling as the neural models. Their
features came from the pipeline of our earlier DLBCL study
\citep{werling2026dlbcl}: using timeseriesflattener
\citep{bernstorff2023tsflattener}, prescriptions, laboratory results,
procedures and referrals, pathology codes, and diagnoses were summarised over 90-day, one-year and three-year windows before
the index date, with aggregates suited to each source (counts and distinct counts for codes, and latest, minimum, maximum, variance and slope for laboratory values). Variables recorded for fewer than 10\% of training patients were
represented by per-source counts of such rare occurrences. To align the register sources available to the tabular
and sequence models, quality-registry variables such as
stage and performance status were removed, and age and sex at the index date
were added. Unlike the earlier study, no LASSO preselection was applied.
Numeric features were median-imputed with missingness indicators and
standardised, and categorical features one-hot encoded, with all preprocessing
fitted on training data. Hyperparameters were chosen for each cell by log loss
on the tuning set from a prespecified grid: for XGBoost, shallow trees (depth
1--3), deeper trees (depth 4 and 6) and the setting of the earlier study
(depth 8, learning rate 0.01, up to 3,000 trees), with the number of trees also
chosen on the tuning set. For logistic regression, the penalty strength was chosen along
a seven-value path ($C$ from $3\times10^{-4}$ to 1, fitted with warm starts). Neither model was class-reweighted. Predicted probabilities were evaluated
without test-set recalibration; absence of class reweighting does not itself
guarantee calibration. \placeholder{Confirm against the
delivered feature export: anchoring at first-line treatment, source list and
number of features.}

As a reference outside the information-matched comparison, XGBoost was also
trained for each cell with the quality-registry predictors of the earlier
study's feature set kept: clinical variables recorded at diagnosis (such as
performance status, stage, lactate dehydrogenase, extranodal involvement and
cytogenetic risk, where the registry for the disease records them) and
registry-recorded adverse events before the index date. They were left missing
where not recorded and handled by the same missingness indicators, and hyperparameters were tuned on the same grid.
The sequence models never saw these variables, so this arm estimates what the
curated registry adds, not a like-for-like comparison.

\operasubsection{National encoder: data and representation}
The national (PHAIR) encoder was pretrained on the registers of the whole
Danish population, converted to MEDS with the same pipeline and the same
processing steps as our cohort. The registers are the same entities, but the
national extract uses older deliveries of some of them. Prescriptions came from an earlier delivery of the prescription register with the same fields. Pathology came from an earlier delivery of the pathology register, which records codes and dates but not the requesting department's specialty or region. Laboratory results came from an earlier research extract of the
laboratory database. The national extract also includes psychiatric hospital
contacts. The code vocabulary and laboratory bins were fitted on the national
data. To use the national encoder, our patients' records were taken from the
national extract and tokenised with its vocabulary, so that it read them in
the form it was trained on. Outcomes, cohort membership and splits were unchanged. The national and cohort encoders therefore differ in population, in
these register deliveries and in training budget, and the comparisons concern
the encoders as delivered. Because the national population contains the cohort,
the national encoder had seen the cohort's pre-2022 records (in the national
deliveries) as well as those of everyone else; continued pretraining on the
cohort adds no patients and is domain adaptation.

In the conversion, codes with fewer than 100 events were merged into their
parent codes. These counts were taken over a random 90\% of all patients in the
extract and over the whole period, so they included events after 2021,
including later events of patients in the test period. Although no outcome labels were used, this representation choice depends on
aggregate code frequencies after the temporal cutoff. It is therefore a
transductive preprocessing step, not a strictly prospective vocabulary design.
Its potential influence is a limitation; restricting token fitting and gradient
updates to pre-2022 events does not undo it. Everything the models learned from was restricted to the
training period: laboratory bins were fitted on events before 2022, the token
vocabulary was built from events before 2022 (codes first seen later became
an unknown token), and pretraining used only events before 2022.

The merging threshold and the binning depend on the size of the dataset they
are applied to, so the two vocabularies differ in resolution as well as in
coverage. With the same threshold of 100 events, the national extract keeps
specific codes, such as particular molecular findings or rare diagnoses, that
the cohort's conversion merged into their parents; and because the number of
bins grows with the number of distinct values, laboratory results are binned
more finely. Finer resolution is one route by which the national encoder, and
any larger dataset, could improve prediction, separate from the knowledge
learned from other patients.

The national encoder was pretrained for 10 epochs with a 1 January 2022
cutoff. Continued pretraining on the cohort (adaptation) and pretraining on the
cohort from scratch ran for the same 10 epochs, with validation-based
checkpoint selection; on tuning data, encoders pretrained for 10 and 20
epochs gave the same fine-tuned performance. Actual
patients, tokens and optimisation steps are reported separately, because equal
epochs do not guarantee equal exposure. \placeholder{Verified national training
population, observation years, events/tokens, encoder version and PHAIR
provenance/reference.}

\operasubsection{Data efficiency of national pretraining}
To test whether population-scale pretraining is most useful when local labels
are scarce, the joint model was retrained from the cohort encoder and from the
national encoder adapted to the cohort on 10\%, 25\% and 50\% of the training
patients, with otherwise identical training. Patients were selected by a fixed
hash of their identifier, so the subsets are nested (10\% within 25\% within
50\%), identical for every outcome and reproducible, and each disease keeps
its expected share. Tuning and test patients were never subsampled, model
selection used the full tuning set, and every model was evaluated on the same test cells. National versus cohort encoders were compared at each share with the same
paired bootstrap as the main analysis.

\operasubsection{Comparison with clinical prognostic indices}
The quality registries record, for each patient, the prognostic index scored at
diagnosis (for example the IPI, FLIPI, MIPI, CLL-IPI or ISS)
\citep{IPI1993,solalceligny2004flipi,hoster2008mipi,cllipi2016,greipp2005iss}
and its risk
category (low, intermediate or high), before treatment started. Every index
recorded for at least 50 patients of a subtype, and every index used in more
than one subtype pooled across all subtypes recording it, was compared with the joint model's prediction for the endpoint the index was developed for, in the patients who had that index (see the end of this section). For DLBCL we also computed the
NCCN-IPI from the components recorded in the lymphoma registry, following
the original definition \citep{zhou2014nccnipi}: age (41--60, 61--75 and
over 75 years: 1, 2 and 3 points), LDH relative to the local upper limit of normal (205~U/L, or 255~U/L from age 70, with ratios above 1 and above 3 scoring 1 and 2 points),
Ann Arbor stage III--IV (1 point), involvement of bone marrow, central nervous
system, liver, gastrointestinal tract or lung (1 point), and performance
status $\geq$2 (1 point). The categories were low 0--1, low-intermediate 2--3, high-intermediate 4--5 and high $\geq$6. Patients missing any component were not scored.

Model risk groups were fixed on tuning patients with known two-year status and
applied unchanged to the test set. The low cutoff was the highest predicted
risk at which the group below it, of at least 20 patients, had an observed risk
no higher than the index's low-risk category. The high cutoff was the predicted
risk at which the model's specificity equalled that of the index's high-risk
category, so that both flagged the same proportion of patients without the
event. This is also the operating point marked in the precision--recall comparison. Patients between the two cutoffs were divided in the proportions
of the index's intermediate categories (one for the registry indices, two for
the NCCN-IPI). Anchoring both ends to the index makes the groups directly
comparable while leaving the model free to differ: a model that ranks patients
better places more patients at low risk at the same risk, and captures more
events at the same specificity. For each index and each system we report group
size, observed risk, and the sensitivity and specificity of classifying a
patient in that group or higher, and, for the model, the observed risk in its
highest-risk 5\% and 10\% of patients, which the index cannot resolve within
its top category. As a sensitivity analysis, model groups were instead given exactly the index's group sizes. Overall survival was used for the IPI, NCCN-IPI, CLL-IPI, ISS, R-ISS, FLIPI
and MIPI. Separately, treatment failure was used as a proxy for the IPS,
which was built for freedom from progression \citep{hasenclever1998ips}.
Second-line treatment or death is a proxy for progression-based endpoints,
not the same endpoint; the IPS comparison is labelled as endpoint-approximate
and is not combined with endpoint-matched overall-survival comparisons. The horizon was the longest of two years and one year that lay within the median follow-up of the index's test population, estimated by the reverse Kaplan--Meier method and chosen without reference to outcomes. The original indices were developed for longer horizons, usually five years, so even endpoint-matched comparisons use a shorter horizon and are not
reproductions of the original analyses. Discrimination used the index category (or
NCCN-IPI points) as an ordinal score.

\operasubsection{Routing formulation and selection}
To test whether a joint model benefits from conditioning its predictions on
disease and outcome beyond ordinary shared learning, we compared the joint
model with a routed model trained on the same panel. In the routed model, the
patient representation
$h_i$ is adjusted for each target outcome $k$ by a weighted combination of
small residual adapters, one per disease and one per outcome:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 h_{ik}&=h_i+\lambda\sum_s\pi_{iks}\,c_s\,A_s(h_i),\\
 A_s(h)&=U_s\,\mathrm{GELU}(V_s h),
 \qquad c_s=\frac{e_s}{e_s+\tau}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:router}
\end{equation}
where $A_s$ is a low-rank adapter for source $s$, the weights $\pi_{iks}$ are
computed from the patient, the patient's disease and the target outcome, and
$c_s$ shrinks the contribution of sources with few training events $e_s$. The
adapted representation enters the same linear head as in the joint model. Each adapter was
updated only by the supervision of its own disease or outcome. The approach
relates to mixtures of experts and parameter-efficient adapters
\citep{jacobs1991adaptive,ma2018mmoe,houlsby2019adapters,pfeiffer2021adapterfusion,hu2022lora}.

The router configuration was chosen on the tuning set in a short prespecified
sequence: which adapter banks to include (none, disease, outcome or both), then
the adapter rank, then the form of the routing query. A simpler configuration
was preferred unless a more complex one improved tuning performance beyond a
prespecified margin, and a routed model with no adapters was required to
match the joint model before differences between adapter banks were interpreted.

already has its own prediction head, an outcome adapter bank must add value

and are not interpreted as evidence of relationships between diseases.





\operasubsection{Hierarchy for effect heterogeneity}
The primary evidence is the paired effect in each disease--outcome cell with
its patient-bootstrap interval. Many cells are small, so their raw estimates
are noisy and rankings of individual cells are unreliable. We therefore also
fit a deliberately simple Bayesian hierarchical model whose purpose is partial
pooling: each cell's effect is the panel mean plus a disease effect, an outcome
effect and a cell-specific deviation, so that a noisy cell is shrunk towards
what similar cells show, by an amount the data determine. The model answers
two questions: how large is the typical effect, and how much does it vary
between diseases and between outcomes? The cell estimates are not treated as
independent: their sampling covariance, taken from the shared patient-bootstrap
draws, enters the likelihood, because the same patients contribute to every
outcome within a disease.

Whether effects depend on the amount of training data available for the
target is a separate question, and we do not assume a linear relationship.
A low-dimensional spline still constrains smoothness and complexity. The cell effect is allowed to vary smoothly with log training
events through a cubic spline with few knots and a shrinkage prior, to regularise departures from a flat relationship. The prior still affects the fitted shape and is assessed in sensitivity checks. As a transparent check
that does not depend on smoothing, cells are also grouped into prespecified
bins of training events (fixed before test-set evaluation) and the mean effect
is estimated within each bin. Within each
question, the model fits the independent effects directly (for pooling:
disease pooling, outcome pooling and their interaction). Conditional and combined contrasts are exact sums of these, so all reported pooling contrasts
are mutually consistent. Priors
are weakly informative on each metric's scale and checked with prior predictive simulation, and convergence and posterior predictive checks are reported. Posterior probabilities of benefit, harm and practical equivalence
use a prespecified tolerance. Raw paired estimates are always shown alongside.

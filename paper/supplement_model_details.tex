\operasubsection{Data sources and processing}
All data were held and processed in the DALY-CARE environment at the Danish
National Genome Center \citep{brieghel2025dalycare}. Registers were linked
through the civil registration number and converted to the Medical Event Data
Standard (MEDS) with ehr2meds (\url{https://github.com/FGA-DIKU/ehr2meds}).
MEDS stores every record as an event with a patient, a time, a code and an
optional value, so all sources share one format. \Cref{tab:meds-sources} lists
the sources, what each contributes and when its events are dated, and
\cref{fig:model-inputs} shows how they form one sequence per patient.

The registers arrive as separate tables with their own identifiers and
conventions for time, so the conversion first gives them a common form.
Diagnoses and procedures are recorded against a hospital contact rather than a
patient, so they were joined to their contact. Contacts, pathology requisitions
and hospital medication orders were assigned a region and a primary specialty
from the national register of health-care organisations, using each
organisation as it was registered on the date of the record, because
departments merge, move and change specialty over time. The change of patient
register in 2019 also changed how some contact attributes are coded; where a
documented correspondence exists, older values were mapped to their newer
equivalents, so that a token means the same before and after the change.

The time given to an event determines what the model can see at the start of
treatment. The patient registers do not record when during a contact a
diagnosis was made, so diagnoses were placed at the end of their contact, the
first moment they are certain to have been known; a diagnosis made during an
admission that spans the start of treatment is therefore not visible to the
model. Attributes known when a contact opens were placed at its start,
procedures when performed, prescriptions at dispensing, hospital medication at
the start of the order and laboratory results at sampling.

Rare codes give the model too few examples to learn from. The lymphoma
conversion configuration uses adaptive truncation with a default minimum of
100 events. Less frequent diagnosis, procedure, medication and pathology codes
are mapped to the deepest permitted prefix that meets that count. The
permitted prefix lengths depend on the source. Codes without a qualifying
prefix retain their full form. Laboratory and contact codes are not truncated.
A shared pathology prefix need not retain the molecular-result specificity of
the original code. Attribution concepts therefore require the appropriate
result-specific token, checked against the saved mapping, rather than inferring
a positive result from a mixed prefix. Laboratory values were binned
separately for each test: values were clipped at the 1st and 99th percentiles
and assigned to quantile bins, with more bins for tests with more distinct
values, following the power law that Montgomery et al.\ fitted for transformers
on health records ($B = \mathrm{round}(1.14\,N_{\mathrm{distinct}}^{0.237})$,
between 2 and 100) \citep{montgomery2026numeric}. Each result became one token
combining the test with its bin, or with its text value for non-numeric results
such as ``$<$0.1''. In the lymphoma configuration, bins and code counts are
fitted on events before 1 January 2022 from a random 90\% of patients. This
split serves only these statistics and is unrelated to the study's training,
tuning and test periods. \placeholder{Confirm the frozen build configuration
and retain its adaptive-code mapping and numeric metadata with the model.}

BONSAI \citep{montgomery2025bonsai} then turned each patient's events into the
sequence the models read. The vocabulary was fitted on events before 1 January
2022, and codes first seen later were read as an unknown token, so no code that
appears only in the test period has a learned embedding. Sex and age opened the
sequence, a separator marked each change of time so that events recorded
together stay grouped, and every event kept its time. For each prediction the
sequence was cut strictly before the start of treatment and, if longer than
8,192 tokens, shortened to the sex and age tokens and the most recent events.
The conversion can be rerun on the same registers inside the DALY-CARE
environment from the configurations in the ehr2meds repository.

\operasubsection{Model development}

\paragraph{Encoder and pretraining.} The encoder builds on CORE-BEHRT
\citep{odgaard2024core}, adapted from a bidirectional to a causal transformer
\citep{vaswani2017attention} that predicts each next event from the events
before it, with every event's time encoded by a scaled Time2Vec representation
\citep{kazemi2019time2vec} (\cref{tab:model-spec}). It was pretrained on the
histories before 1 January 2022 of the pretraining population, every DALY-CARE
patient: adults diagnosed with a lymphoid cancer or a precursor condition (MGUS,
MBL) since 2002 (78,668), for 10 epochs, the budget of the national encoder. An encoder twice as
wide was considered before the analysis was locked and not adopted: fine-tuned
as the joint model, it improved tuning AUC by 0.003, within the difference
between two fits of the same model, so the narrower encoder was kept. This encoder initialised
every supervised model, each trained, tuned and tested on the final
cohort (37,531 patients).

\paragraph{Sampling and loss weighting.} Patients were sampled in their natural
proportions, without oversampling patients with events or reweighting classes,
to avoid deliberately changing class proportions. This does not guarantee
calibration, which is evaluated separately. Each training patient has events for a few of the 69 outcomes and
none for most, so no single weighting of patients could balance all
disease--outcome--horizon tasks at once; balance between tasks instead comes
from the loss, averaged so that each endpoint group weighs equally. As a
sensitivity analysis fixed before the test set was opened, the routed model
R\textsubscript{AB} (Supplementary Methods, learned routing) and the joint model were refitted with importance-weighted event sampling: batches were
enriched towards patients with events at the six horizons, and each patient's
loss was divided by their sampling weight, so the likelihood remained that of
the natural population (\result{IPW_R_auc}{X} and \result{IPW_J_auc}{Y} in AUC
against natural sampling).

\paragraph{Memorization check.} Test patients' records before 2022 were part of
pretraining, so we checked whether the encoder reproduces the records it was
trained on. The pretrained model was given the first $X$ events of a patient's
record ($X$ = 16 to 4,096) and continued it greedily for $Y$ events ($Y$ = 1 to
64); the continuation was compared with the real record by four scores: all $Y$
events reproduced in order, the share of the next $Y$ events reproduced in any
order, the same within 30 days of when they occurred, and the share of the
patient's rare codes (held by fewer than 1 in 1,000 training patients)
reproduced. Records are partly predictable, so each score was compared between
up to 2,000 pretraining patients and up to 2,000 patients the encoder never saw,
and memorization was read as the excess of the former, considering only
combinations of $X$ and $Y$ with at least 50 scored patients in each group. The
next-event loss could not tell the two groups apart (AUC 0.505, 95\% CI 0.480
to 0.531), and \placeholder{the largest excess of any score was X (CI) at
$X$ = , $Y$ = } (code: \texttt{bonsai/run/memorization\_check.py} in the
BONSAI repository).

\paragraph{Prediction head and loss.} Every supervised model consisted of the
pretrained encoder, trained further, and a linear head that produced, for each
outcome, piecewise-constant hazards for the outcome and for competing death,
from which the cumulative incidence at each horizon was derived
\citep{gensheimer2019nnetsurvival,lee2018deephit}. The loss was a binary
cross-entropy at each horizon over the patients whose status was known: those
with the event before the horizon were positive, those who died of another cause
or were followed beyond it negative, and those censored earlier did not
contribute at that horizon. Censoring weights were used in evaluation, not in
training. Losses were averaged within each outcome and then across endpoints,
treating the grade variants and nested definitions of one finding as one
endpoint family (69 separately predicted outcomes, 52 endpoint families).
Losses were averaged within each family before families received equal weight,
so that findings represented by several grades or nested definitions did not
receive disproportionate training weight. This grouping affected loss weighting,
not the number of outputs or their separate evaluation. In \opera{}, each
outcome's reading of the record and its corrections lie between the encoder and
this head (Supplementary Methods, outcome-specific reading); the head and the loss are those of the
joint model.

\paragraph{Supervision scopes.} The four scopes, described in Methods, differed
only in which cells of the disease-by-outcome grid provided labels
(\cref{fig:supervision-grid}). Every model
predicted all six horizons and was trained with the same settings
(\cref{tab:model-spec}) on the same target patients, labels and horizons. Each
selected its checkpoint on the tuning labels within its own scope, so a
contrast between scopes includes what the extra labels do to training as well
as to the final prediction. A disease without tuning patients therefore cannot
have a model of its own: nodal marginal-zone lymphoma had five training patients
and none in 2022 (\cref{tab:characteristics}), so its specialists and its
outcome-pooled model were not estimable by design and were recorded as such,
not as failures; its predictions come only from the disease-pooled and joint
models and \opera{}. Because equal epochs do not mean equal computation,
optimisation steps and compute are reported for each scope.

\operasubsection{Outcome panel}
We predicted 69 outcomes, each at 30, 60, 90, 180, 365 and 730 days, in 12
clinical domains (definitions supplement): disease course and later malignancy
(including second-line treatment, treatment failure, transformation, new
malignancy and death); infection and antimicrobial use; erythroid findings and
transfusion; neutrophil, white-cell and platelet findings; lymphocyte and
eosinophil counts; renal injury and support; liver injury and coagulation;
sodium and potassium; calcium and magnesium; metabolic and cell-turnover
markers; cardiovascular and thrombotic events; and acute care and other events.
Laboratory outcomes were defined with reference to the CTCAE laboratory
criteria; for each outcome, the definitions supplement states whether it equals a
CTCAE grade, follows one with a stated difference, or has no counterpart
\citep{nci2017ctcae}.
The laboratory correspondence audit identified 13 outcomes matching CTCAE grade
thresholds, 13 following a grade with a documented deviation, and three without
a direct CTCAE counterpart. Deviations include boundary conventions and omitted
baseline-dependent criteria. Symptom- and intervention-dependent grades cannot
be reproduced from laboratory measurements alone. The ascertainment supplement
provides the version-specific correspondence and implementation qualifications.
Infection was also captured through antimicrobial treatment, labelled as such.
A patient contributed to an outcome only if it could be ascertained for them,
for example if laboratory follow-up was available, and was otherwise left out
rather than counted as negative. Death was a competing event for every non-fatal
outcome. Definitions, code lists and risk-set sizes are given in the
definitions supplement.

\operasubsection{Tabular comparators: features and tuning}
For each disease--outcome cell and horizon, we trained gradient-boosted trees
(XGBoost) \citep{chen2016xgboost} and ridge-penalised logistic regression on the
same patients, labels and competing-event handling as the neural models. Their
features came from the pipeline of our earlier DLBCL study
\citep{werling2026dlbcl}: using timeseriesflattener
\citep{bernstorff2023tsflattener}, prescriptions, laboratory results,
procedures and referrals, pathology codes and diagnoses were summarised over
90-day, one-year and three-year windows before the index date (counts and
distinct counts for codes; latest, minimum, maximum, variance and slope for
laboratory values). Variables recorded for fewer than 10\% of training patients
were represented by per-source counts of such rare occurrences, and comorbidity
by these counts rather than a weighted index such as Charlson. Features were
constructed separately within each disease, so each disease's models used a
feature set suited to its own patients. The XGBoost pooled across diseases used
one matrix constructed in the same way across all supervised patients and, like
the neural pooled models, was not given the disease. To align the register
sources of the tabular and sequence models, quality-registry variables such as
stage and performance status were removed, and age and sex at the index date
were added. Numeric features were median-imputed with missingness indicators and
standardised, and categorical features one-hot encoded, with all preprocessing
fitted on training data. Hyperparameters were chosen for each cell by log loss
on the tuning set from a prespecified grid: for XGBoost, shallow trees (depth
1--3), deeper trees (depth 4 and 6) and the setting of the earlier study
(depth 8, learning rate 0.01, up to 3,000 trees), with the number of trees also
chosen on the tuning set; for logistic regression, seven penalty strengths.
Neither model was class-reweighted or recalibrated. \placeholder{Confirm against
the delivered feature export: anchoring at first-line treatment, source list and
number of features.}

As a reference outside the information-matched comparison, XGBoost was also
trained for each cell with the quality-registry predictors of the earlier
study kept: variables recorded at diagnosis (such as performance status, stage,
lactate dehydrogenase, extranodal involvement and cytogenetic risk, where the
registry for the disease records them) and registry-recorded adverse events
before the index date, left missing where not recorded. The sequence models
never saw these variables, so this arm estimates what the curated registry
adds, not a like-for-like comparison.

\operasubsection{National encoder: data and representation}
The national (PHAIR) encoder was pretrained on the registers of the whole
Danish population, converted to MEDS with the same pipeline. The registers are
the same, but the national extract uses older deliveries of some of them:
pathology without the requesting department's specialty and region, and an
earlier research extract of the laboratory database; it also includes
psychiatric contacts. Its vocabulary and laboratory bins were fitted on the
national data. To use it, our patients' records were taken from the national
extract and tokenised with its vocabulary, so that it read them in the form it
was trained on; outcomes, cohort membership and splits were unchanged. Because
the national population contains the cohort, the national encoder had seen the
cohort's pre-2022 records as well as everyone else's, and continued pretraining
on the cohort adds no patients: it is domain adaptation. The national and
cohort encoders therefore differ in population, register deliveries and
training budget, and the comparisons concern the encoders as delivered.

With the same threshold of 100 events, the national conversion keeps specific
codes, such as particular molecular findings or rare diagnoses, that the
cohort's conversion merged into their parents, and bins laboratory results more
finely. Finer resolution is one route by which the national encoder, or any
larger dataset, could improve prediction, separate from what it learns from
other patients. In the national conversion, rare codes were counted over the
whole period, including events after 2021. No outcomes were involved, and
everything the models learned from was restricted to events before 2022, but
which codes kept their own token depends on these later frequencies; we note it
as a limitation.

The national encoder was pretrained for 10 epochs with a 1 January 2022
cutoff. Continued pretraining on the pretraining population (adaptation) and
pretraining on it from scratch ran for the same 10 epochs; on tuning data,
encoders pretrained for 10 and 20 epochs gave fine-tuned performance within the
difference between two fits of the same model. As references, a randomly
initialised encoder of the same architecture was trained with the same
supervised protocol, and the pretrained cohort encoder was kept frozen while
only the prediction head was trained.
Patients, tokens and optimisation steps are reported separately, because equal
epochs do not guarantee equal exposure. The national encoder was trained on
7,668,683 people and validated on a further 852,071, a split of people for
pretraining only and unrelated to the study's tuning and test sets. On
validation, the next recorded event was its most probable prediction
\placeholder{50}\% of the time, among its 10 most probable \placeholder{95}\% and
among its 100 most probable \placeholder{98}\%. \placeholder{Observation years,
events and tokens, encoder version and PHAIR reference.}

\operasubsection{Data efficiency of national pretraining}
To test whether population-scale pretraining is most useful when local labels
are scarce, the joint model was retrained from the cohort encoder and from the
national encoder adapted to the cohort on 10\%, 25\% and 50\% of the training
patients, with otherwise identical training. The subsets are nested (10\% within
25\% within 50\%), identical for every outcome, and keep each disease's share.
Tuning and test patients were never subsampled, model selection used the full
tuning set, and national and cohort encoders were compared at each share with
the paired bootstrap of the main analysis.

\operasubsection{Comparison with clinical prognostic indices}
The quality registries record, for each patient, the prognostic index scored at
diagnosis (for example the IPI, FLIPI, MIPI, CLL-IPI or ISS)
\citep{IPI1993,solalceligny2004flipi,hoster2008mipi,cllipi2016,greipp2005iss}
and its risk category. Every index recorded for at least 50 patients of a
subtype was compared with \opera{}'s prediction for the endpoint the
index was developed for, in the patients who had that index, and every index
used in several subtypes also across all of them, since the IPI, for instance,
is applied to many rare lymphomas without a score of their own. For DLBCL we
also computed the NCCN-IPI from its components in the lymphoma registry,
following the original definition \citep{zhou2014nccnipi}: age (41--60, 61--75
and over 75 years: 1, 2 and 3 points), LDH relative to the upper limit of
normal (above 1 and above 3 times: 1 and 2 points), Ann Arbor stage III--IV,
involvement of bone marrow, central nervous system, liver, gastrointestinal
tract or lung, and performance status $\geq$2 (1 point each), grouped as low
(0--1), low-intermediate (2--3), high-intermediate (4--5) and high ($\geq$6).
Patients missing a component were not scored.

Model risk groups were developed using the fitted model's training-cohort
predictions at the designated endpoint and horizon, then applied unchanged
to tuning and test patients. Index event risks and matching specificity were
censoring-weighted within the score-available training population. These are
apparent training predictions, not out-of-fold estimates. Matching in training
does not guarantee matched risks or specificity in evaluation. The low
cutoff was the highest predicted risk at which the group below it, of at least
20 patients, had an observed risk no higher than the index's low-risk category.
The high cutoff approximately matched the specificity of the index's high-risk category, so
that both flagged the same share of patients without the event; this is also
the operating point marked in the precision--recall comparison. Patients in
between were divided in the proportions of the index's intermediate categories.
A model that ranks patients better can then place more patients at low risk at
the same observed risk, and capture more events at the same specificity. For
each index we report group sizes, observed risks, and the sensitivity and
specificity of classifying a patient in a group or higher, and, for the model,
the observed risk in its highest-risk 5\% and 10\%, which the index cannot
resolve within its top category. As a sensitivity analysis, model groups were
given exactly the index's group sizes.

Overall survival was the endpoint for the IPI, NCCN-IPI, CLL-IPI, ISS, R-ISS,
FLIPI and MIPI. The IPS, built for freedom from progression
\citep{hasenclever1998ips}, was compared on treatment failure, a proxy that is
reported separately as endpoint-approximate. The horizon was two years where at
least a third of the index's test patients could have been followed for two
years (judged from index dates and the data cutoff alone, without reference to
outcomes), and one year otherwise; one year is reported alongside. Censoring
before the horizon is handled by inverse probability of censoring weighting, so
the criterion concerns precision, not bias. The original
indices were developed for longer horizons, usually five years, so these are
shorter-horizon evaluations, not reproductions of the original analyses.
Discrimination used the index category (or NCCN-IPI points) as an ordinal
score.

\operasubsection{Outcome-specific reading and own corrections}
\label{sec:opera-formulation}
\opera{} keeps the joint model's encoder, loss and prediction head, and changes
only what lies between the encoder and the head. Let $H_i=(h_{i1},\dots,h_{iT})$
be the encoder's final token states for patient $i$, and $s_i=h_{iT}$ the state
of the token appended after the record. In the joint model, $s_i$ is the
summary passed to the head for every outcome. In \opera{}, each outcome $k$
first reads the record through its own learned query $q_k$, and what it reads
is added to the shared summary through a learned gate $g_k$:
\begin{equation}
 r_{ik}=s_i+\tanh(g_k)\,\mathrm{MHA}\!\left(q_k;H_i\right).
 \label{eq:opera-reading}
\end{equation}
Here MHA is multi-head cross-attention (8 heads, dropout 0.1) with $q_k$ as the
query and the token states as keys and values. The appended token is excluded
from the keys, so that a query cannot simply copy the shared summary. The size
of the gate, $|\tanh g_k|$, is the weight the outcome gives to its own reading.
Small low-rank adapters \citep{houlsby2019adapters,hu2022lora} then correct
each outcome's representation:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 z_{ik}&=r_{ik}+\lambda\left[A^{\mathrm{G}}(r_{ik})+A^{\mathrm{D}}_{d(i)}(r_{ik})+A^{\mathrm{O}}_{k}(r_{ik})\right],\\
 A(r)&=U\,\mathrm{GELU}(V r).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:opera-corrections}
\end{equation}
$A^{\mathrm{D}}_{d(i)}$ is the adapter of the patient's diagnosis $d(i)$,
$A^{\mathrm{O}}_{k}$ that of the outcome, and $A^{\mathrm{G}}$ one global adapter
shared by every task. Each adapter has rank 8 ($V$ maps the 256-dimensional
representation to 8 dimensions and $U$ back), and $\lambda=0.7$ is a fixed
residual scale. A diagnosis adapter enters every outcome of the patients with
that diagnosis and is trained by all of them; an outcome adapter enters, and is
trained by, every patient eligible for that outcome. A patient whose diagnosis
has no adapter would receive the global correction only. The corrected
representation $z_{ik}$ enters the same linear competing-risk head as in the
joint model, which gives the hazards of outcome $k$ and of competing death.
Each disease--outcome task therefore keeps the shared encoder, head and global
adapter, and specialises in two places only: what its outcome reads ($q_k$,
$g_k$) and the corrections of its diagnosis and its outcome.

Every fit starts at the joint model. The gates start at zero, so that
$r_{ik}=s_i$ exactly; the queries and the adapters' $V$ start from normal
distributions with standard deviation 0.02, and $U$ with standard deviation
0.001, so that the corrections start close to zero. At the first step each
outcome's representation is therefore the shared summary, and the prediction is
that of a joint model with the same encoder and head; each task departs from it
only as far as training moves it. All parameters were trained together with the
joint model's loss and protocol (\cref{tab:model-spec}), with AdamW. The
encoder was trained at 0.1 times the learning rate of the other parameters, as
in every other supervised model; the queries, the attention projections, the
gates, the adapters and the head were trained at the full rate
(\placeholder{learning rate}). The smaller optimiser constant used for the
routing parameters of the router (Supplementary Methods, prespecification) does
not apply. Fits with the encoder at the full rate are a sensitivity analysis
(Supplementary Methods, design search and selection). \placeholder{Parameters added to the joint model by
the reading and by the corrections.}

The designs of the search (\cref{tab:design-search}) change
\cref{eq:opera-reading,eq:opera-corrections} in a few ways. With tied queries,
each query is a combination of 8 shared basis queries plus its own deviation,
$q_k=\sum_m c_{km}b_m+q'_k$, with $q'_k$ starting at zero. With shrinkage, the
diagnosis and outcome terms of a patient with diagnosis $d$ are multiplied, for
outcome $k$, by $n_{dk}/(n_{dk}+\tau)$, where $n_{dk}$ is the number of training
events in that disease--outcome cell and $\tau$ is fixed (20, 50 or 100), not
learned. With a diagnosis $\times$ outcome term, each outcome rescales the
diagnosis adapter's bottleneck, $U_d\,[\mathrm{GELU}(V_d r)\odot(1+\sigma_k)]$,
with $\sigma_k$ starting at zero, so that the diagnosis correction can differ by
outcome while its adapters stay shared. With lineage or endpoint-family
corrections, one further adapter per lineage or per endpoint family is added at
full weight to every member and trained by all of them, without shrinkage.

\operasubsection{Learned routing (tested alternative)}
\label{sec:router-variants}
\label{sec:learned-routing}
Learned routing was the original design of \opera{} and is reported here as a
tested alternative. In the routed model, the shared summary $s_i$ is adjusted
for each target outcome $k$ by a weighted combination of small residual
adapters, one per source $s$:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 h_{ik}&=s_i+\lambda\left[G(s_i)+\sum_s\pi_{iks}\,c_s\,A_s(s_i)\right],\\
 A_s(h)&=U_s\,\mathrm{GELU}(V_s h),
 \qquad c_s=\frac{e_s}{e_s+\tau}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:router}
\end{equation}
$A_s$ is a low-rank adapter of rank 8, $\lambda=0.7$ the residual scale, $c_s$
shrinks sources with few training events $e_s$ ($\tau=10$ events), and $G$ a
global adapter outside the routed mixture. The sources are the diseases and the
outcomes. The weights $\pi_{iks}$ come from a softmax over the sources and one
further slot that applies no adapter; they depend on the patient, the patient's
disease and the target outcome (an interacting query of width 512), and the
patient's own disease always receives at least 0.05. Each adapter is updated
only by the supervision of its own source; when another task borrows it, its
parameters are used but not updated. The router is held fixed for the first two
epochs. This is R\textsubscript{AB}. The approach relates to mixtures of
experts and to combining adapters
\citep{jacobs1991adaptive,ma2018mmoe,pfeiffer2021adapterfusion}.

The first router, configured on the tuning set by sources, rank and query in
turn, routed poorly. Most routing weight sat on one or two sources whose
corrections were small, while the own disease adapters, which enter every
prediction at the 0.05 floor, had grown corrections about 20 times larger; any
weight borrowed from another disease therefore injected a large correction
learned for that disease, and the router parked its weight on a source that
changed little. Two changes, fixed with their controls before they were trained
(26 October 2026), addressed this: the global adapter outside the mix, and two
epochs in which the router is held fixed while the adapters train. Routing was
judged healthy when, measured by contribution rather than weight, the most
frequent top source led in at most half of the rows, at least three different
borrowed sources led across diseases, and the median effective number of
sources exceeded 1.5. R\textsubscript{AB} routed healthily on both seeds, and an
amendment made before any test data were opened (27 October 2026) made it the
reference router; the first router is a sensitivity analysis
(\texttt{R\_ORIG}). A sensitivity fit of the first router that started the slot
without an adapter at a larger share (logit 3.0 against 0) is reported in the
same way.

Later stages asked whether routing could add to own corrections by any
reasonable mechanism. R\textsubscript{O} applied the own disease and outcome
adapters at full weight outside the mix, which then held only borrowed sources;
R\textsubscript{PP} weighted the own corrections by $n/(n+\tau)$, with $\tau$
learned, and soft-normalised every correction; R\textsubscript{PT} routed by
task rather than by patient, with bounded logits. Both capped the own
corrections that carried R\textsubscript{AB}'s gain, and both fell below it.
Stage 8 kept the own corrections exactly as in
R\textsubscript{O} and pooled only what was borrowed, and every design in it
contains own corrections alone (\texttt{R\_PF\_OFF}) as the special case of
borrowing nothing (\cref{tab:design-search}).

Learned routing is reported as a null. The gain of the routed models over the
joint model came from the own corrections. Against an own-only control that
kept the own adapters inside the routed mixture, R\textsubscript{AB} gained
$+0.014$ in tuning AUC (95\% interval 0.001 to 0.027); with the own corrections
at full weight outside the mix, borrowing added $+0.0016$ ($-0.0006$ to 0.0042;
R\textsubscript{O} against its own-only control); the stage-8 designs differed
from R\textsubscript{AB} by \placeholder{range}; and R\textsubscript{AB} against
own corrections alone was \placeholder{\texttt{AB\_OFF}, tuning}.
R\textsubscript{AB} is scored
on the test set as the step in which learned routing replaces each outcome's own
reading on the same corrections (\cref{tab:panel-effects}). The analyses that
describe a router's internals (route weights, source removal, routing health and
route concentration) do not apply to \opera{} and were not run for it.

\operasubsection{Hierarchy for effect heterogeneity}
Claims rest on the pooled paired-bootstrap differences and their labels
(\cref{tab:panel-effects}); the hierarchical model is descriptive. Many cells
are small, so their raw estimates
are noisy and rankings of individual cells are unreliable. We therefore also
fit a simple Bayesian hierarchical model for partial pooling: each cell's
effect is the panel mean plus a disease effect, an outcome effect and a
cell-specific deviation, so that a noisy cell is shrunk towards what similar
cells show, by an amount the data determine. It answers two questions: how
large is the typical effect, and how much does it vary between diseases and
between outcomes? Because the same patients contribute to every outcome within
a disease, the cells' sampling covariance, taken from the shared bootstrap
draws, enters the likelihood.

Whether effects depend on the amount of training data for the target is a
separate question, and we did not assume a linear relationship: the cell effect
varies smoothly with log training events through a cubic spline with few knots
and a shrinkage prior towards a flat relationship. As a check that does not
depend on smoothing, cells were also grouped into bins of training events fixed
before test-set evaluation. Pooling contrasts were fitted as their independent
parts (disease pooling, outcome pooling and their interaction), so that every
reported combination is an exact sum and all are mutually consistent. Priors
are weakly informative on each metric's scale and checked by prior predictive
simulation; convergence and posterior predictive checks are reported, and
posterior probabilities of benefit, harm and practical equivalence use a
prespecified tolerance and are descriptive, not claim labels. Raw paired estimates are always shown alongside.

\operasubsection{Statistical inference and descriptive analyses}
Time-dependent AUC was the prespecified primary performance metric. Brier score,
IPA and calibration provided complementary assessments of probability accuracy.
Each pooled difference was labelled by a rule fixed before testing: better (or
worse) if its 95\% interval lay above (below) zero, noted as beyond the practical
margin when the difference exceeded 0.01 AUC; otherwise equivalent if the
interval lay within $\pm$0.01; otherwise inconclusive; each with a paired
bootstrap $P$ value. The headline comparisons form one family, with $P$ values
adjusted by Holm's method: sharing across diseases, across outcomes and both
(each against the specialists), the joint model and \opera{} against per-task
XGBoost, borrowing by the routed model R\textsubscript{AB} (against its
no-borrowing control), the national encoder against the cohort
encoder, and \opera{} against the clinical indices. Everything else, including
results for individual diseases, outcomes, cells and disease pairs, is reported
as estimates with intervals and is descriptive. Across disease--outcome cells, effects were also summarised,
descriptively, by hierarchical models that estimate the typical effect, its variation between diseases and outcomes,
and its relation to the number of training events; estimates for individual
cells are shown in the Supplementary figures.
Calibration regression estimated the intercept and slope jointly from the
unchanged model probabilities. These coefficients describe calibration and
were not applied to update risks. Fits without a finite solution, including
separation of events and non-events, were reported as not estimable.
Medians and interquartile ranges describe variation between cells, rather than
uncertainty in a pooled mean. Compute, training time and storage were compared
for one shared model and the separate models it replaces. Integrated gradients
and their event-removal and randomisation checks are described in the
interpretability supplement \citep{sundararajan2017ig,adebayo2018sanity}.

The generated tuning recipe required at least 50 evaluated patients per
clinical-score illustration. This is an operational display threshold, not a
sample-size justification or a guarantee of adequate events within score
strata. Group counts and uncertainty remain necessary. Crossed or coincident
matched thresholds, or thresholds leaving empty training strata, were reported
as not estimable rather than artificially separated.

\operasubsection{Temporal differences between study periods}
The three periods differed in ways that a temporal evaluation has to absorb.
National laboratory capture is complete only from 2017, so patients treated
earlier had far fewer recorded results (median 119 in the training period
against 524 in the test period), and later patients had longer recorded
histories (median 11.6 against 22.0 years). Performance status and the
disease-specific index category, recorded in the quality registries, were
missing for 47\% and 58\% of test patients against 24\% and 36\% in training.
The reason for this difference in registry completeness was not established. The
disease mix shifted too: DLBCL made up 27.5\% of training and 22.7\% of test
patients, while indolent B-cell lymphomas rose from 13.4\% to 18.8\%, partly
through more unspecified B-cell and splenic marginal-zone lymphoma, and test
patients were older (median 72 against 69 years). The test set therefore
measures performance under clear data shift.

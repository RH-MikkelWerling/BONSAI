# Introduction argument and thesis alignment

The introduction's chain is clinical breadth → isolated supervision → unused
neighbouring experience → evidence and unresolved attribution → reusable
representations → matched scope comparisons → external reuse/adaptation → study.
Each paragraph supplies the reason for the next rather than another model list.

Thesis inspiration was read from `phd_repos/thesis/kappa/kappa.tex`, especially
“The argument in brief”, “Prognostic indices”, and the five-information-axes
framework; the borrowing-evidence draft and OPERA direction note were also read.
The thesis files were not edited. October 12 configuration/result notes are
historical context, not evidence for the current experimental protocol.

Carried into the manuscript:

- Available clinical information, rather than model size, is the organising idea.
- Richer patient histories do not automatically overcome scarce outcome labels.
- Disease pooling and outcome pooling are separate information axes; outcome
  labels can add supervision without adding patients.
- Shared predictive patterns do not imply interchangeable diseases/biology.
- Borrowing must be evaluated for harm as well as benefit; scarcity is not a
  guarantee of improvement.
- Linked resources and reusable representations enable further applications,
  but this paper tests predictive reuse, not institutional deployment or efficacy.

EveryQuery is cited using arXiv v3 (26 September 2026), whose title and author list
have changed since the initial version. It is positioned as task-conditioned,
direct clinical prediction without task-specific fine-tuning. OPERA's claim is
not the invention of a model predicting multiple tasks: its main contribution
is the matched disease/outcome supervision factorial, tabular comparison, and
external/direct/DAPT comparison on a curated treatment-start panel. No EveryQuery
performance numbers are transferred to our setting and no zero-shot claim is
made for OPERA.

Clinical motivation also draws on the thesis section “Why predict what will
happen to a patient?”: directing monitoring/supportive-care attention, discussing
prognosis, and studying risk-stratified interventions. The paper frames these
as potential uses, not demonstrated OPERA benefits, and distinguishes prognosis
under observed care from treatment-effect prediction. No unverified contemporary
guideline-specific regimen recommendation was imported from the thesis.

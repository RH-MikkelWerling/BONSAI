\begin{abstract}
Prognostic tools are built for one disease and one outcome, so each learns only
from the events of its own patients, which for many clinical questions are few.
Learning across related diseases and outcomes could recover the signal this
leaves unused, but how much comes from other diseases, how much from other
outcomes, and whether a model can learn what to borrow, and from whom, is largely
unknown. We studied this in the lymphoid cancers, many small diseases with many
important but individually rare complications, still served by simple prognostic
indices.
In 37,531 Danish adults starting first-line treatment for one of 24 lymphoid
cancers, including plasma-cell neoplasms, sequence models read each patient's
records before treatment and predicted 69 outcomes over 30 days to two years.

identical models that learned from a single cell, from one outcome across all
diseases, from all outcomes of one disease, or from the whole grid; a router
that learns, for each patient and outcome, how much to draw on each disease; and
encoders pretrained on the records of the entire Danish population.

\operasection{Introduction}
Medicine has learned to tell diseases apart. Classifications grow finer with
every new entity recognised \citep{alaggio2022who}, and clinical research has,
for good reasons, concentrated on what separates one diagnosis from the next.
Prognostic tools have followed suit: each is built for one disease and one
outcome, apart from the neighbouring diseases and related outcomes that could
inform it. The price is paid in data. Every question learns only from the events
of its own patients, and those are few even where patients are many: most
outcomes are uncommon, events limit what any model can learn \citep{Riley2020},
and flexible models need many more of them than regression
\citep{vanderploeg2014data}. In clinical prediction as elsewhere, performance
grows with data \citep{hestness2017scaling}, yet a model built for one disease
and one outcome leaves most of the available information unused.

Diseases and outcomes are not independent. Patients with different diseases
share physiology, treatments, complications and patterns of care, and the
outcomes of one patient are shaped by the same frailty, prior illness and course
of treatment. Much of reading a record does not depend on the disease at all: an
infection, a decline in kidney function or an emergency admission is recorded
and handled in much the same way whatever the cancer behind it. What one task
learns can therefore inform another, so that each task needs to learn only what
is particular to it \citep{Baxter2000,Maurer2016}. This is the premise of
multi-task learning and of pretraining.

The idea is not new. Multi-task learning trains related predictions together
\citep{caruana1997multitask}, meta-learning approaches such as MetaPred learn
from related clinical tasks how to predict a new one with few patients
\citep{zhang2019metapred,finn2017maml,liu2023taml}, and in oncology a mortality
model shared across eleven cancer types improved when it was then adapted to
each type \citep{Oh2021twostage}. Closest to haematology, a transformer reading
routine laboratory trajectories predicted 162 treatment-associated
complications in multiple myeloma and ovarian cancer, with predictions that
generalised across both \citep{lubberstedt2026labs}. But sharing does not always
help. Multi-task
studies of health records report uneven benefits and negative transfer
\citep{harutyunyan2019multitask,mcdermott2020mtl}, and joint learning has helped
rare phenotypes while hurting common ones \citep{ding2019phenotyping}. Ideally,
a model would not borrow indiscriminately but learn what to borrow, and from
whom, weighing each related disease and outcome by how much it helps the
prediction at hand, much as partial pooling weighs each estimate by its evidence
\citep{EfronMorris1975}. A prediction of infection in a patient with chronic
lymphocytic leukaemia should then draw on what was learned about infection in
multiple myeloma, where the immune deficiency is similar, and set aside what was
learned about relapse in aggressive lymphoma. Mechanisms for selective sharing
exist, from automated grouping of prediction tasks \citep{cui2024autodp} to
mixtures of experts that route patients to specialised subnetworks
\citep{lee2022residualmoe,wang2025moehealth}, but how much they add across the
diseases and outcomes of a clinical specialty has not been tested.

Over the past few years, the wider field of clinical prediction has turned
towards exactly this philosophy: large models trained on the records of many
patients, diseases and outcomes, which could replace the many models built one
task at a time. Self-supervised models such as BEHRT,
Med-BERT, CORE-BEHRT, MOTOR and SurvivEHR learn from recorded histories before
any outcome is labelled
\citep{li2020behrt,rasmy2021med,odgaard2024core,steinberg2023motor,gadd2026survivehr},
and Delphi-2M, life2vec and pan-cancer models learn across diseases and whole
populations
\citep{shmatko2025delphi,savcisens2024life2vec,placido2023pancreatic,Jee2024MSKCHORD,bjerregaard2025pancancer},
as do generative models of medical events trained on a whole health system's
records \citep{waxler2025comet};
promptable models can be asked about any outcome \citep{chandak2026everyquery}.
Trained on the claims of more than 200 million people, one such model recently
outperformed disease-specific gradient boosting across more than a thousand
disease-onset tasks, with the largest gains for rare diseases
\citep{ma2026reclaim}.
One model for many questions would be easier to build, validate and maintain,
and could serve questions too small for a model of their own. Yet such models
are rarely evaluated on outcomes that matter for clinical decisions
\citep{wornow2023shaky}, against the tools in clinical use
\citep{worth2026state}, or on how few labels they need \citep{wornow2023ehrshot}. It remains largely unknown how much is
gained by learning across diseases, how much by learning across outcomes, and
whether a model can learn how much to draw on each.

The lymphoid cancers are a fitting place to ask. They comprise dozens of
entities \citep{alaggio2022who}, each uncommon \citep{sant2010haemacare}:
in the cohort studied here, 24 diseases range from almost 10,000 patients with
diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) to fewer than 50 with amyloidosis, and even
the most common offers few events for most questions. A complication with a
one-year risk of 2\% gives fewer than 200 events among the patients with DLBCL
treated before 2022, and in a disease of 500 patients a risk of 5\% gives 25. At
the same time, these patients share much of their work-up and care: the same
blood counts and biochemistry, imaging, chemotherapy backbones and supportive
care. At the start of treatment, a haematologist weighs treatment failure
alongside infection, cytopenia, organ toxicity and unplanned admission, yet the
tools for this remain simple. Prognostic indices such as the International
Prognostic Index (IPI) and its NCCN refinement, the CLL-IPI and the revised
International Staging System turn a handful of measurements at diagnosis into
points for one disease and one endpoint
\citep{IPI1993,zhou2014nccnipi,cllipi2016,palumbo2015riss}, losing information
that the same measurements carry when kept continuous
\citep{biccler2018simplicity}. They matter nonetheless: the Danish DLBCL
guideline sets the number of cycles and the intensity of treatment by the
age-adjusted IPI \citep{DLG2025DLBCL}, and indices decide entry to trials of new
first-line therapy \citep{Tilly2022POLARIX,Langerbeins2022CLL12}. Machine
learning on the wider record has improved on them
\citep{Biccler2018,agius2020clltim,werling2026dlbcl}, and studies pooling
lymphoid cancers have examined single outcomes \citep{parviz2026dacll}, but these
models share the indices' basic limitation: one disease, one outcome. For many
complications and diseases, no dedicated predictor exists.

DALY-CARE makes it possible to study the lymphoid cancers as a whole. The resource links
ten nationwide registers, electronic health records and laboratory data for all
Danish adults registered with a lymphoid cancer since 2002, including the
plasma-cell neoplasms and their precursors \citep{brieghel2025dalycare}. The
Danish registers, linked through a personal identifier, follow hospital contacts,
prescriptions, laboratory results and deaths across the population
\citep{schmidt2014cpr,schmidt2019danish}, and national quality registries record
staging, treatment and response with near-complete coverage
\citep{Arboe2016lyfo,daCunhaBang2016,Gimsing2016}. DALY-CARE was created in large
part so that the lymphoid cancers could be modelled together, on the expectation
that what they share would improve prediction for each of them.

Taken to its extreme, learning from related tasks need not stop at haematology.
The records of every resident hold diseases, treatments and patterns of care far
beyond any specialty, and an encoder pretrained on them has seen more of how
records unfold than any cohort can show. It can also read records in finer
detail: findings too rare to learn from in a cohort, such as specific molecular
results, are common enough across a whole population to be learned. If such an
encoder can be reused for specialised prediction, one investment in learning
from population records could serve many clinical questions, and specialties
could build on it rather than pretrain their own. Whether it can is an open
question, because a general encoder may not fit a specialised population. The
established remedy is domain adaptation, continued pretraining on the target
population \citep{gururangan2020dont,guo2024multicenter,lemmon2023pediatric}.

We therefore asked how much each of these sources of signal is worth for the
questions a haematologist faces at the start of treatment: other diseases, other
outcomes, learning how much to draw on each, and the whole population; and
whether, together, they improve on the tools haematology uses today. In 37,531
patients with 24 lymphoid cancers, we predicted 69 outcomes at six horizons from
the start of first-line treatment. We compared the approaches in
\cref{fig:paradigms}: the prognostic indices used in the clinic, gradient-boosted
trees tuned for every prediction, as the established improvement from machine
learning, and sequence models pretrained across the cohort, the current
instantiation of learning across diseases and outcomes. To isolate what the
additional information adds, we compared specialist models, each trained on one
disease and one outcome, with models trained across diseases, across outcomes or
both, with a router that learns how much to draw on each disease, and with
encoders pretrained on the whole Danish population. Finally, we asked whether
what the model learns to borrow follows clinical knowledge, against pairs of
diseases expected to share signal and negative controls fixed before testing.

\operasection{Methods}

\operasubsection{Population, prediction time and outcomes}
We included adults in DALY-CARE who started first-line treatment for a lymphoid
or plasma-cell malignancy \citep{brieghel2025dalycare}. Every prediction was made
at the start of first-line treatment, from the diagnoses, procedures, pathology,
medication, laboratory results and hospital contacts recorded before it, with
age, sex and the region and specialty of care (\cref{fig:model-inputs}). The study was approved by the Danish
Health Data Authority (P-2020-561) and the National Ethics Committee (1804410).

We predicted 69 outcomes at six horizons from 30 days to two years, spanning
disease course, infection, cytopenia, organ toxicity, cardiovascular events and
acute care, with death as a competing event. Laboratory outcomes were defined
with reference to the laboratory criteria of the Common Terminology Criteria for
Adverse Events \citep{nci2017ctcae,nci2025ctcae}: 13 equal the thresholds of a
CTCAE grade, 13 follow a grade with a stated difference (for example, a value
exactly at a cut-off counted in the more severe grade, or a baseline arm not
applied), and three, including acute kidney injury, have no CTCAE counterpart;
symptoms and interventions, required by some grades, are not recorded. The
correspondence of every outcome is listed in the Supplementary Methods. Laboratory outcomes were counted
only in patients whose blood was monitored, so that time without testing
did not pass as event-free. Outcome definitions, disease assignment, treatment
lines and ascertainment rules are given in the Supplementary Methods.

\operasubsection{Temporal design}
Patients treated through 2021 formed the training set, those treated in 2022 the
tuning set, on which every modelling choice was made, and those treated from
2023 the held-out test set, which was evaluated once. Pretraining used events
before 1 January 2022.

\operasubsection{Learning across diseases and outcomes}
Every sequence model in this study has the same backbone: a causal transformer,
building on CORE-BEHRT \citep{odgaard2024core}, pretrained to predict each next
event in a patient's record and fine-tuned to give the cumulative incidence of
every outcome at every horizon, with death competing
\citep{gensheimer2019nnetsurvival,lee2018deephit}. Models were trained with a
binary cross-entropy at each of the six horizons, over the patients whose status
at that horizon was known, averaged within each outcome and then equally over
its 52 endpoints (grade variants of one finding together), and were evaluated on
the full risk set with censoring weights (Evaluation). What differs is what each
model learns from (\cref{fig:supervision-grid}). Arranged as a grid of diseases
by outcomes, a specialist learns from a single cell, a disease-pooled model from
one column (one outcome in every disease), an outcome-pooled model from one row
(every outcome in one disease), and the joint model from the whole grid.
Comparing them shows how much a prediction gains from other diseases, from other
outcomes and from both.

Sharing the whole grid has a cost: the joint model represents each patient once,
so every disease and every outcome must make do with the same representation.
The routed model keeps that shared representation and lets each prediction
adjust it, in the spirit of partial pooling (\cref{fig:source-router}). Each
disease has an adapter, or expert: a small correction learned only from its own
patients' labels and shrunk towards zero when it has few events. For every
patient and target outcome, a router decides how much of each disease's adapter
to apply, the patient's own and those of other diseases
\citep{ma2018mmoe,houlsby2019adapters,hu2022lora}. The model thus learns what to
borrow, and from whom. The joint model knows the patient's disease from the
record, but everything it learns about a disease must be built into one encoder
that all diseases share and the largest dominate, and at the readout every
outcome has parameters of its own but no disease has. A disease adapter adds a
patient-by-disease-by-outcome term that the router turns up or down for each
patient. Outcome adapters, which add little to an outcome's own output layer,
were screened as well.

The router was configured on the tuning set in three steps: which adapters
(disease only, outcome only or both), their size (rank 8, 16 or 32), and how the
router combines patient, disease and outcome (additive, or interacting with a
width of 128 or 512). Each step took the option with the highest tuning AUC,
averaged over estimable cells with each disease weighing equally, each outcome
equally within a disease and all six horizons. Options within the difference
between two fits of the same model counted as tied and went to the simplest;
that difference was measured as the router trained with every source masked,
which computes the joint model, against the joint model itself. An option whose
IPA fell short of the simplest option's by more than the same kind of noise
floor was not eligible. The screen chose disease adapters only
\placeholder{rank and interaction}. The sources were screened at the smallest
rank, and the choice may differ at another rank or query; the four adapter banks
(none, disease, outcome, both) are therefore all trained with the locked rank
and query and compared on the test set as a secondary analysis. Three
controls separate borrowing from simpler explanations: a joint model told the
patient's disease, which tests whether disease-specific baseline risks explain
the gain, and routers limited to the patient's own disease adapter (the
own-disease router) or own outcome adapter, which test whether corrections help
through their own parameters, without borrowing. A sensitivity fit starts the
router with a larger share on the shared representation (about 15--18\% of the
weight rather than about 1\%); a rule fixed before its tuning results were seen
decided between the two starts (Supplementary Methods).

The widest grid is the whole population. A model pretrained on the registers of
every Danish resident has learned to read records from far more patients,
diseases and treatments than any specialty can offer. We started the joint and
routed models from PHAIR \placeholder{PHAIR reference}, an encoder pretrained by
its developers on the registers of the entire Danish population, either as
delivered or after continued pretraining on the cohort's records
\citep{gururangan2020dont}. Since the population contains the cohort, the first
comparison measures what the rest of the population adds, and the second what
refocusing on the cohort adds. If the population supplies much of what a model
needs, the model should also need fewer labelled patients: we retrained the
joint model from the cohort encoder and the adapted national encoder on 10\%,
25\% and 50\% of the training patients, and compared the two encoders at each
share and each with the joint model and the specialists trained on all
patients. Randomly initialised
and frozen encoders are references for pretraining and fine-tuning;
architecture, training and the national data are described in the
Supplementary Methods.

\opera{} (\modelname) is the configuration of scope, routing and encoder with
the highest AUC on the tuning set, averaged over diseases, outcomes and all six
horizons; configurations within the difference between two fits of the same
model counted as tied, and the simplest was chosen, the joint before the routed
model and the cohort before the national encoder \placeholder{selected
configuration}. It is the model compared with tabular models and clinical
indices. Because \opera{} was chosen on tuning data, the joint model, fixed before
any tuning result was seen, is compared with the tabular models as well; both
comparisons are reported. The contrasts between scopes, routing and encoders use
their fixed arms.

\operasubsection{Comparators}
We compared all models with tabular comparators, which remain hard to beat on
routinely collected data \citep{grinsztajn2022tabular}. XGBoost
\citep{chen2016xgboost} and ridge logistic regression were tuned for every
disease, outcome and horizon on summaries of the same register histories, built
within each disease with the pipeline of our earlier DLBCL study
\citep{werling2026dlbcl,bernstorff2023tsflattener}. XGBoost pooled across
diseases, on features built across all patients, tests whether pooling also
helps tabular prediction, and XGBoost given the quality-registry variables shows
what curated clinical fields add. Clinical indices were compared with \opera{}
and the tabular models on the patients with the index recorded, on the endpoint
each index was developed for: the IPI and NCCN-IPI in DLBCL
\citep{IPI1993,zhou2014nccnipi} and every other index recorded for at least 50
patients of a disease. Model risk groups were fixed on tuning data to match the
index's low-risk observed risk and high-risk specificity.

\operasubsection{Evaluation}
The primary metric was the time-dependent area under the ROC curve (AUC) at each
of the six horizons. Reported differences and \cref{tab:panel-effects} pool all
six horizons, each disease--outcome--horizon cell weighing equally; the main
figures show one year, except the borrowing analysis, which pools horizons, and
every horizon is in the Supplement. The index of
prediction accuracy (IPA), Brier score and calibration measured probability
accuracy
\citep{blanche2013competingauc,gerds2006brier,schoop2011competing,kattan2018ipa}.
Every contrast was paired on the same patients, with intervals from 1,000
patient resamples within disease, shared across models, outcomes and horizons.
Each pooled difference was labelled by a rule fixed before testing: better (or
worse) if its 95\% interval lay above (below) zero, noted as beyond the practical
margin when the difference exceeded 0.01 AUC; otherwise equivalent if the
interval lay within $\pm$0.01; otherwise inconclusive; each with a paired
bootstrap $P$ value. The headline comparisons form one family, with $P$ values
adjusted by Holm's method: sharing across diseases, across outcomes and both
(each against the specialists), the joint model and \opera{} against per-task
XGBoost, borrowing by the routed model, the national encoder against the cohort
encoder, and \opera{} against the clinical indices. Everything else, including
results for individual diseases, outcomes, cells and disease pairs, is reported
as estimates with intervals and is descriptive. Across disease--outcome cells, effects were also summarised,
descriptively, by hierarchical models that estimate the typical effect, its variation between diseases and outcomes,
and its relation to the number of training events; estimates for individual
cells are shown in the Supplementary figures. Decision curves, in patients with
DLBCL and the IPI recorded, compare the net
benefit of acting on \opera{}, the IPI and XGBoost when patients above a
predicted two-year risk of death receive closer monitoring and supportive care,
across thresholds from 5\% to 40\% \citep{vickers2006dca}, and at two
prespecified points: the model's low- and high-risk cutoffs, fixed on tuning
data by matching the IPI's low-risk observed risk and high-risk specificity. Training time, compute and storage are reported for one
\opera{} model and for the separately trained models it replaces. Integrated
gradients \citep{sundararajan2017ig} describe what \opera{} relies on, by
source, time and clinical finding and for individual patients, checked by event
removal and weight randomisation \citep{adebayo2018sanity}. The choices fixed
before the test set was evaluated are listed in the Supplementary Methods
(Prespecification). Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI \citep{collins2024tripodai}
\placeholder{checklist in the Supplement}.

\operasubsection{Which diseases the model borrows from}
The routed model's readout adds one routed correction per source disease and its
risk head is linear, so a source can be switched off exactly, with every other weight
as trained: its correction is replaced by its average over tuning patients with the
same disease and outcome, which removes what the source says about the
individual patient but keeps the average shift the model was trained with
(mean ablation; setting it to zero is a sensitivity analysis; Supplementary
Methods). One evaluation of the locked model scored, on
the same test patients, the model with all sources, with only the patient's own
disease (no borrowing), with the own disease and one other (the source alone)
and with all sources but one (the source left out). The source alone shows what
a disease contributes on its own, which similar diseases cannot mask; the source
left out shows what only that disease provides, so a disease that adds alone
but costs little when left out shares its information with others. How much the
model relies on borrowing overall is the AUC it loses when all borrowed
corrections are switched off, for each disease, each clinical domain and over all
diseases. Within one fitted model this carries no noise of refitting, but it
measures what the fitted model uses, not what borrowing adds when a model is
trained without it; that is the comparison with the own-disease router. Before the test set was
opened, we listed candidate pairs of diseases with a possible reason to share
signal for a domain of outcomes (shared biology, treatment or complications),
kept only pairs whose estimate could be informative (at least 200 training
patients in the source disease and 50 test patients in the target), and asked
study haematologists to rate each candidate, in random order and without our
rationale. Pairs rated as a strong or moderate source of signal were the
expected pairs, and pairs rated as unrelated the negative controls
(Supplementary Methods). The primary
test asked whether these pairs, as a set, gained more from their source alone
than the typical pair, the mean of the other pairs of the same domains
(permutation test). The map of all pairs is descriptive and partially pooled.
AUC differences were primary and IPA secondary. The analysis concerns borrowing
at the readout; sharing through the common encoder is measured by the joint
against the specialist models.

\operasubsection{Cohort characteristics and outcomes}
The pretraining population comprised all 78,668 adults in DALY-CARE, which
includes every Danish adult diagnosed with a lymphoid cancer or a precursor
condition (MGUS, MBL) since 2002 \citep{brieghel2025dalycare}, whose records before
2022 pretrained the cohort encoder. Of these, 37,531 started first-line treatment with an assigned disease
and form the final cohort (\cref{fig:cohort-flow,tab:characteristics}): 29,105
treated through 2021, 1,980 in 2022 and 6,446 from 2023. The median age was 69
years (IQR 60--77), 41.7\% were women, and the largest diseases were diffuse
large B-cell lymphoma (26.5\%), multiple myeloma (23.0\%), chronic lymphocytic
leukaemia (10.9\%) and follicular lymphoma (7.8\%).

The three periods differed in ways that a temporal evaluation has to absorb.
National laboratory capture is complete only from 2017, so patients treated
earlier had far fewer recorded results (median 119 in the training period
against 524 in the test period), and later patients had longer recorded
histories (median 11.6 against 22.0 years). Performance status and the
disease-specific index category, recorded in the quality registries, were
missing for 47\% and 58\% of test patients against 24\% and 36\% in training,
presumably because registration of the most recent years is incomplete. The
disease mix shifted too: DLBCL made up 27.5\% of training and 22.7\% of test
patients, while indolent B-cell lymphomas rose from 13.4\% to 18.8\%, partly
through more unspecified B-cell and splenic marginal-zone lymphoma, and test
patients were older (median 72 against 69 years). The test set therefore

\operasection{Discussion}
A single model that learned across 24 lymphoid cancers and 69 outcomes
\placeholder{discriminated better than gradient-boosted trees trained and tuned for
every disease, outcome and horizon in X of Y predictions, separated outcomes
more clearly than the prognostic indices in their own populations and on their
own endpoints, and gave predictions for diseases and complications that no index
covers}, and it did so as one model in place of \result{narrow_fits}{N}.
\placeholder{Principal findings with uncertainty: what other diseases, other
outcomes, learning how much to borrow and population pretraining each added;
where the gains were largest; whether what the model borrowed followed clinical
expectation; and the most informative null or harmful result.}
To our knowledge, this is the first study to learn across the diseases and
outcomes of the lymphoid cancers as a whole, and the first to separate how much
is gained from other diseases, from other outcomes, from learning how much to
draw on each and from population pretraining, against both tuned task-specific
models and the indices used in the clinic. The principles come from multi-task
learning and pretraining; what this study shows is how much they are worth for
the questions a haematologist faces at the start of treatment. Recent work has shown the value of shared models for disease onset across
claims \citep{ma2026reclaim} and for complications in myeloma and ovarian cancer
\citep{lubberstedt2026labs}; our results carry this from the onset of disease to
the outcomes of its treatment, across every lymphoid cancer and the full record,
against the clinical tools of the specialty, and show where the gain comes
from. A recent review of clinical AI found no comparison of the large models of
medical histories with the clinical scores in use, and judged that the most
credible progress so far is on narrowly defined tasks with clear endpoints
\citep{worth2026state}. This study aims at both: its 69 outcomes were
prespecified and fixed at the start of treatment, and each was compared with
models tuned for it and, where one exists, with the index used in the clinic.

The controlled comparisons show where shared signal comes from. Another disease
can supply examples of the same complication; another outcome can teach the
encoder the patient's underlying vulnerability; the two can complement each
other, repeat the same information or conflict. \placeholder{Which pattern
occurred here, and how the gain varied with the data a prediction had of its
own.} The asymmetry between the axes matters for how such models should be
built: as described in Methods, the joint model gives every outcome parameters
of its own but no disease any, and disease adapters were chosen on the tuning set
\placeholder{and added more on the test set}, so the room for improvement
lies in letting each disease adjust a shared representation rather than in
pooling more outcomes. Sharing
everything is not guaranteed to help: multi-task studies of health records have
reported uneven benefits and negative transfer
\citep{harutyunyan2019multitask,mcdermott2020mtl,ding2019phenotyping}, and
pooling related aggressive lymphomas has matched but not clearly improved on a
model trained on DLBCL alone \citep{werling2026dlbcl}. \placeholder{Whether and
where sharing everything hurt here, and whether learning how much to borrow
recovered it.}

This runs against how haematology has organised prognosis. The field has
advanced by telling diseases apart: each new entity in the classification,
each index built for one disease and validated in its trials, and each
registry and study group organised by disease rests on the assumption that
prognosis is disease-specific. That assumption is often right about treatment
and biology. \placeholder{If the results hold: but for predicting what happens
to a patient after treatment starts, the patients of other diseases were
informative, and a prediction for one disease improved when the model learned
from all of them, also in the common diseases, where a specialised model had
the most data of its own.} \placeholder{If the borrowing analysis agrees: what
the model drew on included diseases far apart in the classification that share
complications or treatment, such as chronic lymphocytic leukaemia and myeloma for
infection (\cref{fig:borrowing-map}C).} The boundaries that matter for diagnosis and
treatment need not be the boundaries that matter for prediction, and treating
them as such leaves information unused, most of all in the diseases with the
fewest patients.

Routing is one way to let a model learn how much to borrow; meta-learning
\citep{zhang2019metapred}, automated task grouping \citep{cui2024autodp},
mixtures of experts \citep{lee2022residualmoe,wang2025moehealth} and priors over
related tasks are others. The routing
controls locate the gain \placeholder{if the results hold}: it did not come from
knowing the diagnosis or from the adapters' added capacity, but from borrowing
\placeholder{(with its claim label)}. Where it helped is as informative as how
much, and two mechanisms predict different places. A disease's own correction
should matter most for outcomes whose meaning differs between diseases, such as
progression, second-line treatment and transformation; borrowing from other
diseases should matter most where complications are shared, such as infection
and cytopenia after similar treatment. \placeholder{Which domains relied most
on borrowing} (\cref{fig:borrowing-map}D, \cref{fig:supp-borrowing-detail}D). Switching sources off in
the fitted model goes further than route weights, which show where the router sends
weight but not what that weight contributes. That what the model borrowed
\placeholder{followed} the pairs expected from biology, treatment and
complications, tested as a set against expectations fixed before the test set
was opened and against negative controls, \placeholder{suggests that it found
shared prognostic signal rather than arbitrary correlation}; pairs it relied on
that clinicians would not have predicted \placeholder{examples} are hypotheses
worth testing, not findings about the diseases. Borrowing at the readout is also
only part of the sharing: \placeholder{the fitted router depended on its
borrowed corrections by X AUC points, against Y for pooling across diseases
(the disease-pooled against the specialist models)}, so most of what diseases share is carried by the common
representation, and routing adds a targeted layer on top \placeholder{if the
results hold}.

The most consequential change may be in coverage. Prognostic tools in
haematology exist where a disease is common enough to have one and where its
inputs were recorded: 10 of the 24 diseases have no dedicated, validated index,
and in the test period the index category was missing for 58\% of patients,
because quality-registry fields arrive late. A model that reads the routine
record gives every patient an estimate at the start of treatment, for every
outcome, in every disease, including nodal marginal-zone lymphoma, which had
five training patients and none in the tuning year, so that no model of its own
could even be fitted, and \result{cells_low_support}{N} disease--outcome
combinations with fewer than 20 training events. \placeholder{Performance in
these cells, and in the diseases without an index
(\cref{tab:panel-effects,fig:supp-pooling-cells}).} \placeholder{If the smallest
diseases also gained most from borrowing (\cref{fig:borrowing-map}B,
\cref{fig:supp-borrowing-detail}B), their predictions rest on what other diseases
taught the model rather than on their own few events.} For such questions the
choice is not between a shared model and a better specialised one, because the
specialised one cannot be built.

The same holds over time. The test period brought new first-line treatment
\placeholder{confirm for Denmark: e.g. polatuzumab vedotin in DLBCL, targeted
agents in CLL, daratumumab combinations in myeloma} \citep{Tilly2022POLARIX},
and the records themselves changed between the training and test years: the
national patient register moved from LPR2 to LPR3 in 2019, laboratory capture
was complete only from 2017, and quality-registry fields were increasingly
missing. Shifts of this kind are a common cause of failure for
clinical prediction models \citep{finlayson2021shift}. \placeholder{If
performance held, this supports robustness over the observed period}, although
not to future changes or to another health system. New treatments also start
with few treated patients. If population pretraining and sharing reduce the
labelled patients a model needs \placeholder{the joint model from the adapted
national encoder reached the full-data cohort-encoder model with X\% of the
training patients}, a model can follow a new regimen or a new outcome after
fewer patients have been treated, rather than years later.

Population pretraining takes sharing to its limit, and its result may matter
most beyond haematology. \placeholder{Whether the national encoder helped
directly, only after adaptation, or not at all, and with how few labelled
patients it matched the cohort encoder.} A gain need not come only from what the
encoder learned from other patients: a national extract also represents each
record at finer resolution, keeping specific codes, such as molecular findings,
that a cohort-sized vocabulary merges. \placeholder{If the national encoder
helped:} the encoder was not built for haematology, its developers never saw
these outcomes, and it improved specialised prediction in a cohort that is a
small fraction of the population it learned from. That sets a precedent for
every clinical specialty in Denmark. The national registers record the
diagnoses, procedures, prescriptions, laboratory results and contacts of the
whole population, so a model pretrained once on them could be the starting
point for cardiology, nephrology, psychiatry or any specialty with data of its
own, each adapting it to its patients and labelling only its own outcomes,
rather than building and pretraining a model from scratch. The specialties and
diseases with the fewest patients, which cannot pretrain a model of their own,
would gain most, and a reduction in the labelled patients needed would make the
gain larger still. Whether it holds in other specialties is for those studies to
show, but the infrastructure to test it exists for the whole country.
\placeholder{If it did not help: what this says about the value of specialised
pretraining, and whether the finer vocabulary compensated.} Shared models also
change the cost of prediction. One \opera{} model replaced
\result{narrow_fits}{N} specialists \placeholder{and N tabular models, at X of
their combined compute and storage}, and is one pipeline to validate, monitor
and update rather than thousands; a new outcome needs a new output head rather
than a new project. Because the registers are national, one model could serve
every hospital if it works equally well in every region, which it
\placeholder{did / did not} (\cref{fig:supp-regions}).

For patients, a shared model gives a profile of risks at the start of treatment
rather than a single prognostic summary. A patient at low risk of treatment
failure may be at high risk of serious infection, and prophylaxis, monitoring
and dose planning differ accordingly. A profile is useful only if its
probabilities are accurate, which is why calibration is reported alongside
discrimination, and more useful if a clinician can see what each risk rests on:
a patient's explanation can show, as in the synthetic example, that a high risk of
serious infection follows a recent pneumonia while a high risk of death follows
the findings of the diagnostic work-up (\cref{fig:attribution-patient}). Where the model places
patients at low risk who are on standard treatment, its predictions support
that treatment rather than change it. Where it identifies patients at high
risk, the evidence needed depends on what is done with them
\citep{blythe2026economic}: closer monitoring and supportive care, the action
in the decision curves, asks for little, whereas changing treatment needs a
trial. The model can define who enters such a trial, as CLL-TIM selects
patients for PreVent-ACaLL \citep{Agius2024clltim,preventacall}, and several of
the outcomes predicted here, such as serious infection and severe neutropenia,
are what prophylaxis trials would aim to prevent. Whether explanations of this
kind help clinicians decide needs testing with them
\citep{ghassemi2021explainability}.

This study has limitations. Validation was temporal within one health system,
and test patients' records before 2022 contributed to self-supervised
pretraining, without outcome labels, so the evaluation concerns later treatment
episodes rather than unseen individuals. A memorization check found no sign
that the pretrained encoder reproduces the records it was trained on: its
next-event loss could not tell training from unseen patients (AUC 0.505,
95\% CI 0.480 to 0.531), and its continuations of a patient's record were not
systematically closer for training patients than for unseen ones
(Supplementary Methods); another health system would need to
adapt and validate its own model. Every choice was made on a tuning year of
1,980 patients, small for the rarest diseases, which we addressed by keeping
choices few and rule-based. Each configuration was trained once; the difference
between two fits of the same model \result{R0_auc}{X} indicates the variation
this omits. Outcomes were derived from registers: laboratory abnormalities are
CTCAE-graded measurements rather than adjudicated adverse events, laboratory
risk sets condition on monitoring, transformation is a new diagnosis rather than
a histological finding, and diseases coded after treatment started were assigned
up to 90 days later. Models were trained on patients with known status at each
horizon, which tends to overestimate risk where censoring is common; all models
share this, but calibration at long horizons should be read with it in mind.
Indices could only be compared where they were recorded, mostly as categories
rather than scores, and the recorded patients may differ from those without;
the cutoffs matched to them were estimated on the tuning year and carry
uncertainty that the intervals do not include. The tabular and sequence models
used the same patients and index dates but differ in representation and history
windows, so their comparison estimates the value of complete pipelines. The
national and cohort encoders differ in population, register deliveries,
vocabulary and training budget, so their comparison concerns the encoders as
delivered rather than scale alone. Region was an input, and two regions
contribute richer hospital data, so region partly marks which data a patient
has; performance is reported within each region. The models predict risk under
the care patients received, not the effect of changing it \citep{vanGeloven2020},
and attributions describe the fitted model, not causes. Likewise, the borrowing
analysis describes how the fitted model uses each source at its readout:
removing a source alters a model trained with all of them rather than retraining
without it, sharing through the encoder is not resolved by disease, and pairs
involving the smallest diseases are imprecise. Finally, the study does
not measure whether decisions or outcomes improve when the predictions are used,
evidence that remains rare for clinical AI in general \citep{worth2026state};
a model deployed in practice would also need its inputs from hospital systems at
the time of the decision rather than from registers updated with a delay
\citep{Agius2024clltim}.

\placeholder{Conclusion. Draft if the results hold: prognosis in the lymphoid
cancers does not have to be built one disease and one outcome at a time. One
model that learned across their diseases and outcomes, and how much to draw on
each, borrowed most where clinicians would expect shared signal, discriminated
better than models tuned for each task, gave predictions
where no tool exists, and held up over a period of changing treatment and
registers. A model pretrained on the whole population improved it further,
which opens the same route to every specialty that shares the national

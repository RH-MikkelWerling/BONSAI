\begin{abstract}
Prognosis shapes treatment choice, supportive care and the decisions patients
make about their future. Yet most prognostic tools learn from one disease and
one outcome at a time. This leaves potentially useful information unused, as
related diseases share biology, treatments and complications that may inform
prediction across diseases and outcomes. We use lymphoid cancers as a proving
ground for whether these relationships can improve prediction, particularly
when individual tasks have few events. Specifically, we ask what learning
jointly across diseases and outcomes adds, whether task-specific corrections
limit negative transfer, and whether nationwide pretraining improves
specialised prognosis.

We studied 37,531 Danish adults starting first-line treatment for 24 lymphoid
cancers, predicting 69 outcomes at six horizons from 30 days to two years using
routinely recorded pre-treatment histories. To estimate the effect of learning
across diseases and outcomes, we matched sequence models trained on one
disease--outcome task, across diseases, across outcomes or across both. We also
trained \opera{}, which combines joint learning with disease- and
outcome-specific corrections. It allows predictions to depart from the shared
model where supported by the data, while drawing on other tasks where local
information is scarce. We extended cross-disease sharing beyond haematology
through nationwide pretraining. We distinguished cohort-pretrained
\opera{}-L from nationally pretrained \opera{}-N and its cohort-adapted
version, \opera{}-NA. We compared pretraining strategies and label efficiency,
and evaluated models against per-task and pooled XGBoost and clinical
prognostic indices. Test patients started treatment from 2023.

\operasection{Introduction}
Better prognosis can help clinicians choose treatment, anticipate complications
and discuss the future with patients. In haematology, these predictions are
often made using disease-specific tools. The International Prognostic Index
(IPI), NCCN-IPI, CLL-IPI and revised International Staging System each address
a particular disease setting and endpoint
\citep{IPI1993,zhou2014nccnipi,cllipi2016,palumbo2015riss}. Tailoring a model to
one cohort allows it to reflect that disease, but restricts learning to the
events observed within it. For rare diseases and uncommon complications, there
may be few such events \citep{Riley2020}. Flexible machine learning models can
require more data than simpler regression models
\citep{vanderploeg2014data}. Performance often improves with additional
training data \citep{hestness2017scaling}, but recruiting substantially more
patients with the same disease and outcome may be impractical. Learning from
related diseases and outcomes offers another source of information.

The usefulness of that information depends on what patients and tasks share.
Biologically distinct cancers can involve similar investigations, treatments
and complications. Frailty and prior illness affect several outcomes at once.
Other regularities extend across the whole population. The relationship
between an injury, its investigation and its treatment does not depend on
whether a patient has cancer. A model could learn these patterns from many
patients and use them to improve a prediction for which few labelled examples
exist \citep{Baxter2000,Maurer2016}. Differences between tasks can also make
sharing harmful. MetaPred improved prediction with limited target data by
learning from related clinical tasks \citep{zhang2019metapred}. Another study
found benefits from multi-task pretraining followed by task-specific
fine-tuning, but frequent negative transfer from joint training
\citep{mcdermott2020mtl}.

Sharing need not treat every task alike or average away important differences.
Partial pooling combines shared and task-specific estimates, with their balance
determined by the available evidence \citep{EfronMorris1975}. Neural task
grouping and mixtures of experts offer related ways to adjust sharing
\citep{cui2024autodp,lee2022residualmoe}. The clinical question is whether this
flexibility improves individual predictions while limiting harm from
inappropriate sharing.

Sharing information more widely also motivates the move towards large models
in clinical prediction. BEHRT, Med-BERT and MOTOR learn from longitudinal
records before adaptation to specific tasks
\citep{li2020behrt,rasmy2021med,steinberg2023motor}. Population models extend
this learning beyond a single specialty
\citep{savcisens2024life2vec,shmatko2025delphi}. Nationwide pretraining could
share knowledge of medical histories with cohorts too small to learn those
patterns independently. However, large models have often lacked evaluation
against clinical tools, on decision-relevant outcomes and with limited labels
\citep{wornow2023shaky,worth2026state,wornow2023ehrshot}. They may also require
continued pretraining on the target population
\citep{gururangan2020dont}. Whether national pretraining adds to sharing within
a specialty needs to be tested separately.

Lymphoid cancers provide a proving ground for sharing across diseases and
outcomes. Their many distinct entities are individually uncommon
\citep{alaggio2022who,sant2010haemacare}, yet share substantial elements of
investigation and care. At treatment initiation, clinicians consider disease
control alongside infection, cytopenia, organ toxicity and admission. Existing
indices inform treatment and trial eligibility, but address only part of this
risk profile \citep{DLG2025DLBCL,Tilly2022POLARIX,Langerbeins2022CLL12}.
DALY-CARE links nationwide registers, health records and laboratory data for
Danish adults with lymphoid cancers, including plasma-cell neoplasms
\citep{brieghel2025dalycare}. It allows these prediction questions to be studied
together within the same population.

We study 69 outcomes at six horizons in 37,531 patients with 24 lymphoid cancers.
Using matched models, we distinguish the contribution of each source of shared
information (\cref{fig:paradigms}). We organise the investigation around four
questions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \emph{How well can one model predict across the specialty?}
  We assess predictability and compare sequence models with task-specific
  tabular methods and established prognostic indices.
  \item \emph{What does sharing within the specialty add?}
  Matched models isolate learning across diseases, outcomes and both.
  OPERA tests whether disease- and outcome-specific corrections improve joint
  learning and limit negative transfer.
  \item \emph{What does learning beyond the specialty add?}
  We compare cohort and national pretraining, test cohort adaptation and
  measure how much labelled data each strategy requires.
  \item \emph{What do predictions depend on, and where are they reliable?}
  Source removal and attribution examine learned sources and recorded events.
  Sensitivity analyses assess recent-record gaps and variation across patients,
  regions and time.
\end{enumerate}

\operasection{Methods}

\operasubsection{Population, prediction time and outcomes}
We included adults in DALY-CARE who started first-line treatment for a lymphoid
or plasma-cell malignancy \citep{brieghel2025dalycare}. Every prediction was made
at the start of first-line treatment. Inputs included diagnoses, procedures,
pathology, medication, laboratory results, hospital contacts and their recorded
attributes, including region and specialty of care, before treatment. Age and
sex were also included (\cref{fig:model-inputs}). The study was conducted under the DALY-CARE approvals from the Danish Health
Data Authority (P-2020-561) and the National Ethics Committee (1804410).

We predicted 69 outcomes at six horizons from 30 days to two years, spanning
disease course, infection, cytopenia, organ toxicity, cardiovascular events and
acute care, with death as a competing event. Laboratory outcomes followed the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse
Events (CTCAE), the standard framework for grading adverse events in oncology
trials, including haematology \citep{nci2017ctcae,nci2025ctcae}. CTCAE grades
link abnormalities to clinically recognisable levels of severity, from mild
findings to events requiring intervention or urgent care. We followed its
laboratory thresholds as closely as the available routine data permitted.
Adaptations where symptoms, interventions or baseline measurements were
unavailable, and outcomes without a CTCAE equivalent, are documented in the
Supplementary Methods. These are CTCAE-based laboratory findings, rather than
adjudicated treatment-related adverse events.
Laboratory outcomes were counted
only in patients whose blood was monitored, so that time without testing
did not pass as event-free. Outcome definitions, disease assignment, treatment
lines and ascertainment rules are given in the Supplementary Methods.

\operasubsection{Temporal design}
Patients treated through 2021 formed the training set, those treated in 2022 the
tuning set, on which every modelling choice was made, and those treated from
2023 the held-out test set, which was evaluated once. Pretraining used events
before 1 January 2022.

\operasubsection{Learning across diseases and outcomes}
We organised supervised learning as a grid with one row per disease and one
column per outcome (\cref{fig:supervision-grid}). Each cell is a prediction
question, such as the risk of infection in a patient with DLBCL, evaluated at
six horizons. To isolate the contribution of related tasks, we trained four
matched models with different supervision scopes. A specialist used labels
from one cell. A disease-pooled model used one column, learning one outcome
across diseases. An outcome-pooled model used one row, learning all outcomes
within a disease. The joint model used the whole grid. These comparisons test what is
gained from supervision across diseases, across outcomes and across both.

The sequence models shared a causal transformer backbone, building on
CORE-BEHRT \citep{odgaard2024core}, pretrained to predict the next recorded
event. Fine-tuning estimated cumulative incidence at each horizon, with death
as a competing event for non-fatal outcomes
\citep{gensheimer2019nnetsurvival,lee2018deephit}. All 69 outcomes were predicted and evaluated separately. Training and
loss-weighting details are given in the Supplementary Methods.

In addition to the joint model, we trained Outcome Prediction with Expert
Routing Across diseases and outcomes (\opera{}). It allows the balance between
sharing and specialisation to vary by patient and target outcome
(\cref{fig:source-router}). A shared encoder represents the patient's history.
Small trainable modules, called adapters, provide disease-specific and
outcome-specific corrections. A router weights these corrections using the
patient representation, disease and target outcome
\citep{ma2018mmoe,houlsby2019adapters,hu2022lora}. Inspired by partial pooling
\citep{EfronMorris1975}, the architecture retains a shared prediction path while
allowing task-specific adjustments and contributions from other tasks. It aims
to pool where sharing is useful and specialise where differences matter.
Whether this improves prediction and limits negative transfer is tested in
the comparisons below.

We used controlled model variants to examine what these corrections add.
Comparison with the joint model measures their overall contribution. Models
restricted to the patient's own disease and target-outcome corrections test
whether task-specific adjustment alone is sufficient. Disease-only and
outcome-only adapter variants distinguish the two sources of adjustment.
Finally, removing individual source corrections from the fitted model shows
which sources its predictions depend on. These analyses make it possible to
examine the contribution of sharing beyond the routing weights themselves.
The removal analysis describes dependence within the fitted model, rather
than a causal relationship between diseases.

We distinguish cohort-pretrained \opera{}-L, nationally pretrained \opera{}-N
and the cohort-adapted national version, \opera{}-NA. Unless qualified,
\opera{} refers to the primary cohort-pretrained model in the local comparisons.
Architecture, tuning, router selection and sensitivity variants are detailed
in the Supplementary Methods.

\operasubsection{Learning from national records}
National pretraining extends information sharing beyond patients with the
cancers studied here. It includes people without these diseases, whose records
may appear unrelated to the target predictions. Their histories can still
contain general patterns of illness, investigation and treatment that may help
an encoder interpret the records of patients with cancer. This tests whether
information from outside the specialty improves specialised prognosis. We pretrained PHAIR, a causal transformer
encoder, on nationwide Danish register histories before 1 January 2022, and
used it to initialise the joint and routed models. We compared this national
encoder with the cohort-pretrained encoder, and also continued national
pretraining on the cohort's records before fine-tuning
\citep{gururangan2020dont}. These comparisons assess population pretraining
and subsequent adaptation to the specialty. Because register deliveries,
vocabularies and training exposure also differed, they compare the complete
pretraining strategies rather than isolating population size alone
(Supplementary Methods).

To assess whether national pretraining reduced the need for labelled data, we
fine-tuned joint models from the cohort encoder and the adapted national
encoder on 10\%, 25\% and 50\% of the training patients, comparing them at each
share and with models trained on the full cohort. Randomly initialised and
frozen-encoder models provided references for pretraining and fine-tuning.
National data, representation, training budgets and sampling procedures are
described in the Supplementary Methods.

\operasubsection{Comparators}
We compared the sequence models with XGBoost \citep{chen2016xgboost} and
ridge logistic regression. Tabular models provide strong baselines that are
difficult to outperform on routinely collected data
\citep{grinsztajn2022tabular}. Features summarised the same pre-treatment
histories over 90-day, one-year and three-year lookback windows. Code-based
features included counts and distinct counts, while laboratory features included
the latest value, minimum, maximum, variance and slope
\citep{bernstorff2023tsflattener}. This aggregation approach previously improved
risk stratification over established prognostic scores in DLBCL
\citep{werling2026dlbcl}. We tuned a separate model for each disease, outcome
and horizon. Additional XGBoost models pooled patients across diseases or
included quality-registry variables to assess the contribution of pooling and
curated clinical information.

Comparisons with prognostic indices included patients with the corresponding
score available across the diseases in which it was recorded. Each contrast identified the sequence model, index,
endpoint and horizon, retaining native scores and risk categories where
available. Model risk-group thresholds were developed from training-cohort predictions
and fixed before evaluation on tuning or test patients. These thresholds
matched the index's training-cohort low-risk event rate and high-risk
specificity. We evaluated recall, precision, specificity, negative predictive
value, MCC, accuracy and F1 at every risk-group boundary. Tuning comparisons
remained exploratory because that cohort also informed model selection. Score definitions, availability, support requirements and threshold
estimation are detailed in the Supplementary Methods.

\operasubsection{Evaluation}
We assessed discrimination using time-dependent area under the ROC curve (AUC)
at each of the six horizons, and probability accuracy using the Brier score
and index of prediction accuracy (IPA)
\citep{blanche2013competingauc,gerds2006brier,schoop2011competing,kattan2018ipa}.
Calibration curves were shown at one year. Calibration slopes and intercepts
were estimated from the original predicted risks using censoring-weighted
logistic regression of outcome status on log-odds of predicted risk.
This assessed calibration without modifying the predictions.

The comparisons outlined in the Introduction used paired predictions on the
same eligible patients. We reported mean differences across supported
disease--outcome--horizon cells, alongside disease-balanced summaries giving
each disease equal weight, outcomes equal weight within disease and horizons
equal weight within outcome. Confidence intervals came from 1,000 patient
bootstrap resamples within disease, shared across models, outcomes and
horizons. Horizon-specific results were reported separately
(\cref{fig:atlas,fig:supp-horizons}). Support criteria, aggregation, multiplicity
adjustment and descriptive analyses are specified in the Supplementary Methods.
Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI \citep{collins2024tripodai}
\placeholder{checklist in the Supplement}.

\operasubsection{Dependence on disease-specific corrections}
We examined whether predictions depended on corrections learned for other
diseases, beyond the information already shared through the encoder. For each
patient, we replaced a selected source disease's correction with its average
among tuning patients with the same target disease and outcome. This removed
the source's patient-specific variation while retaining its average contribution.
The encoder, other corrections and model weights were unchanged. The change
in performance measured dependence on that source's varying contribution.
Replacing the correction with zero provided a sensitivity analysis.

We compared the full model with three versions. One replaced all other-disease
corrections with their averages. Another retained a single other-disease
correction alongside the patient's own disease correction. The third replaced
one other-disease correction while retaining the rest. These comparisons
measured overall dependence on other diseases, the contribution of a source
when used alone, and its contribution when other sources remained available.
The outcome-specific corrections were retained throughout.

A source could be useful alone but add little when similar information was
available from other sources. We therefore reported both comparisons rather
than interpreting either as a unique measure of importance. These analyses
concern the fitted model's readout. They do not measure what a retrained model
would learn without a source, or identify the origin of information already
shared through the encoder. Disease-pair maps, lineage summaries and the
concentration of source contributions were descriptive analyses. Their
construction and uncertainty estimates are detailed in the Supplementary Methods.

\operasubsection{Cohort characteristics and outcomes}
The local pretraining population comprised all 78,668 adults in DALY-CARE,
including Danish adults diagnosed with a lymphoid cancer or a precursor
condition (MGUS, MBL) since 2002 \citep{brieghel2025dalycare}. Their records
before 2022 were used to pretrain the cohort encoder. The national encoder
was pretrained on the wider Danish population (Supplementary Methods). Of these, 37,531 started first-line treatment with an assigned disease
and form the final cohort (\cref{fig:cohort-flow,tab:characteristics}): 29,105
treated through 2021, 1,980 in 2022 and 6,446 from 2023. The median age was 69
years (IQR 60--77), 41.7\% were women, and the largest diseases were diffuse
large B-cell lymphoma (26.5\%), multiple myeloma (23.0\%), chronic lymphocytic
leukaemia (10.9\%) and follicular lymphoma (7.8\%).

measurements, and more often lacked registry-recorded clinical scores than
training patients. These differences included changes in registry coverage

\operasection{Discussion}
Learning across diseases and outcomes improved the sequence models relative
to training each task separately \placeholder{confirm the direction and size of
the held-out pooling effects}. That improvement is distinct from outperforming
a strong tabular baseline. \previewonly{In the current tuning analysis, the
cohort-pretrained OPERA model remained below per-task XGBoost in most evaluated
cells.} \placeholder{Report the final OPERA--XGBoost difference and its interval,
and the clinical-index findings separately.} The national-pretraining
comparison remains unresolved. Its results will determine whether population
records improve this balance, rather than being assumed to do so.

The study connects two questions that are often evaluated separately: whether
one model can address many clinical tasks, and what additional information
improves each task. Within the same population, we compared supervision from
one disease, one outcome and both. We then tested task-specific corrections
and population pretraining. Related studies have examined shared prediction
across diseases and complications
\citep{ma2026reclaim,lubberstedt2026labs}. Here, the focus is prognosis from the
start of treatment across a haematological specialty. Comparisons with tuned
tabular models and established clinical indices place the value of sharing
against practical alternatives. These are different benchmarks, and success
against clinical indices would not remove a deficit against XGBoost.

For the first question, the clinical value depends on both predictive accuracy
and the decision the prediction would inform. NCCN-IPI provides the main DLBCL
comparison. \placeholder{Interpret the observed expansion of the low-risk group
relative to NCCN-IPI, together with its mortality and confidence interval.}
A larger low-risk group would identify more patients with favourable outcomes
under the treatment they received. It would not establish that treatment could
safely be reduced. Likewise, identifying more high-risk patients could support
studies of monitoring or prophylaxis, but requires prospective evidence of
benefit before changing care \citep{vanGeloven2020,blythe2026economic}.
Where XGBoost performs better, its accuracy remains a substantive advantage.
The convenience of one shared model must be weighed against that difference,
its calibration and the cost of maintaining the alternatives.

For the second question, the comparisons separate learning across diseases
from learning across outcomes. \placeholder{Interpret which axis contributed
most, its uncertainty, and where the pattern differed.} If other diseases
provide useful examples of the same complication, their labels can improve
prediction without requiring the diseases to share a diagnosis. Benefits from
outcome sharing would instead support learning common information relevant to
several risks. A small additional gain from one axis need not mean that those
tasks are unrelated. They may provide information already learned through the
other axis. Negative effects also matter, particularly where pooling improves
the average but worsens individual tasks. Uneven benefits and negative transfer
have been reported in other clinical multi-task studies
\citep{harutyunyan2019multitask,mcdermott2020mtl,ding2019phenotyping}.

These comparisons revisit the disease boundaries around which prognosis is
usually organised. Classifications distinguish entities with different biology
and treatment \citep{alaggio2022who}. Prognostic indices and disease registries
reflect those distinctions. They do not imply that all useful predictive
information is disease-specific. \placeholder{If supported, describe gains
from other diseases, including whether these extend to common diseases.}
The boundaries needed for diagnosis and treatment may therefore differ from
the boundaries most useful for prediction. This distinction matters especially
for rare diseases, where a dedicated model has little supervision available.

Partial pooling offers a way to retain those distinctions while using shared
information \citep{EfronMorris1975}. OPERA approaches this through a common
representation with weighted disease- and outcome-specific corrections.
\placeholder{Interpret the measured contributions of own-task corrections and
borrowing, including harmful effects and their relationship to training
support.} Broad borrowing could be useful even without strong preference for
particular source diseases. The relevant question is whether the balance of
shared and task-specific information improves prediction. Routing is one way
to learn that balance. Meta-learning, automated task grouping and mixtures of
experts offer alternatives
\citep{zhang2019metapred,cui2024autodp,lee2022residualmoe,wang2025moehealth}.
Our controls assess the contribution of the routed corrections, rather than
establishing that routing is the best method for partial pooling.
Source removal measures dependence within the fitted model. It does not show
that the same source is necessary after retraining, or that borrowing reflects
a biological relationship.

The third question concerns information beyond the specialty. National
pretraining tests whether learning from population records improves specialised
prediction, directly or after adaptation. \placeholder{Interpret the observed
transfer and label-efficiency results, whether positive, null or harmful.}
If fewer labelled patients are needed, population pretraining could make
specialised models more feasible in small cohorts. If it adds little, the
result would favour closer attention to domain fit and supervised learning
within the cohort. The comparison cannot attribute an effect to population
size alone. National and cohort pretraining also differ in record deliveries,
vocabulary and training exposure. Potential applications in other specialties
require their own evaluation.

The final question concerns what predictions depend on and where they remain
reliable. \placeholder{Interpret the attribution and recent-record exclusion
results, including the effect of hiding the last weeks before treatment.}
Dependence on recent measurements may be clinically informative, but also
makes performance sensitive to the timing and completeness of data collection.
Attributions describe the fitted model rather than causes of outcomes
\citep{ghassemi2021explainability}. Performance across years and regions can
identify weaknesses under the changes observed here. It cannot establish
robustness to future treatments or another health system
\citep{finlayson2021shift}. Wider coverage is also a qualified benefit: a shared
model can produce estimates where no dedicated predictor exists, but sparse
outcome data may still prevent reliable validation. Prediction availability
should therefore be distinguished from demonstrated accuracy.

The main limitations concern validation, outcome measurement and precision.
Evaluation was temporal within one health system. Test patients' pre-2022
records could contribute to self-supervised pretraining without outcome labels,
so the test concerns later treatment episodes rather than entirely unseen
individuals. External validation remains necessary. Register-derived outcomes
are also imperfect measures of clinical events. CTCAE-based laboratory
findings are not adjudicated treatment-related toxicities, and their risk sets
condition on monitoring. These choices affect the populations to which the
estimates apply. Finally, the tuning year was small for rare diseases and
clinical-score strata. This limits model selection and risk-group threshold
estimation, and the reported test intervals do not include all uncertainty
from these choices. Limited independent training repeats also leave uncertainty
about reproducibility across fits. Clinical-index comparisons apply to patients
with scores available, who may differ from those without them. Additional
implementation qualifications are given in the Supplementary Methods.

The findings address whether prognosis can benefit from learning beyond one
disease and one outcome at a time. \placeholder{Conclude with the supported
pooling, routing and national-pretraining findings, without implying superiority
to XGBoost unless demonstrated.} Shared prediction is useful only where its
accuracy, calibration and practical advantages justify its use. The next step
is to validate the supported applications externally and test whether acting
on the predictions improves patient care.

National Genome Center \citep{brieghel2025dalycare}. The protocol and the
agreement template are available with the DALY-CARE documentation.
